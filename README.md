# Shipvivor

Take the cruiser *Breakwater* through two coastal patrols and a final dreadnought intercept. Run `cargo run --locked` with Rust 1.97.1 or newer and a graphical desktop. The window opens maximized on the screen it was last on, which is kept in `window.txt` in Shipvivor's settings folder, or in the file `SHIPVIVOR_WINDOW_FILE` names. From the title menu, choose **New run** to pick your first patrol on the chart, **Helm trial** to practice the original buoy course, or **Customise** to pick the flag Breakwater flies.

## At the helm

Breakwater’s captain stands on the forecastle as the ship cruises past the title camera. At sea, her portrait sits beside the telegraph. She flinches when the hull takes a hit or a spotter raises the alarm, braces through hard turns and engine trouble, and grins when an enemy ship goes down.

Each main mount has a labelled sight circle showing where that particular gun is pointing at the selected range. Pan with the trigger released to lay the guns in advance. A green circle is ready, amber is training, blue is reloading, and grey means the ship is in the way. Each fitted barrel has a pip beside the circle. An arrow at the edge of the view indicates an offscreen mount. The same markers follow the zoomed sight.

A faint line traces the flight of a round from each main mount that can bear, rising and falling to the sight and stopping where the round would strike rock, ice, a building or a ship. The mount that fires next draws a little stronger. A main battery round carries 780 metres, and faster rounds carry farther in step with their muzzle speed. As the sight nears that limit, a dotted amber line fades in on the water at the guns' greatest reach either side of the sight's bearing. Aim past it and the lines show the rounds coming down on the dotted line, short of the sight. Both are hidden in torpedo view.

The **main battery** board above the helm instruments is a plan of Breakwater, bow up. Its turrets sit at their fitted positions and train with the real mounts, each barrel fills from the gunhouse out as it reloads, and a gold diamond on the rim marks where the sight is looking. Beside the plan, each fitted barrel has a load bar and reads READY, TRAINING, MASKED or its reload in tenths of a second. A gold arrow marks the barrel a click will fire next. The ready mounts take turns, and each mount cycles through its loaded barrels. The last line counts the loaded barrels on target. A click waits up to 0.4 seconds for a mount that is still training or about to load; the board reads STANDING BY until it fires.

Breakwater's twin gunhouses take their visual cue from the interwar light cruiser HMAS *Perth*. Breakwater starts with one twin mount at each end. The calibre tree runs through 4, 6, 8, 12, 15 and 18 inches. A wider hull supports larger gunhouses. Adding barrels to a turret takes up some of that room. Each main mount trains and loads independently. A heavier gunhouse trains and elevates more slowly: 18 degrees a second at 4 inches, about 13 at 8 inches and 8.5 at 18 inches. Stock reload takes 1.6 seconds per inch of that mount’s effective calibre, so light wing guns can reload while a heavy centreline gun is still loading. Quick Loaders improve both. A field refit offers the next bore or barrel count only when the ship can carry it. Each smaller shielded gun on the beams gives ships first priority. If its firing arc is clear of ships, the crew tracks the nearest visible enemy factory, shore gun or drydock within its fitted range. A bluff or another building can mask the target. The crew shifts fire when a structure goes down. Raider deck guns turn faster than the cruiser's main battery, while the shore turrets turn more slowly. These timings are tuned for the patrol's compressed scale. [Australian War Memorial photograph of Perth’s twin mounts](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C380081)

The main battery crosses 250 metres in about 0.7 seconds. A round rises and falls under game-scaled gravity, can pass over a nearer boat, and throws up a white column when it lands short or long. The shell body scales to the gun that fired it. A short tracer helps you follow small rounds at patrol distances.

Sound takes time to cross the water. You see a distant gun flash, or your own round land, before you hear it: about half a second for a raider firing at 85 metres and nearly three seconds for a splash at the limit of the sight. Distance takes the crack of a gun first and leaves the low roll. A pause or a fitting choice holds sound in the air along with the battle.

Every report also comes back off the sea, rolls away across open water, and returns a second or more later from the land and ice in earshot. The eight biggest and nearest answer: bases, islands and any iceberg ten metres or more across, with a glacier throwing back up to twice what a base island does. Sound from one side reaches the far ear a little late and a little duller, which is clearest on headphones. Your own guns are felt through the deck before their blast reaches the bridge, and for a moment afterwards everything else is dulled.

Shells are heard in flight. They travel faster than sound, so a round passing close arrives silently: first the crack of its shock, then the rush of its flight falling away in pitch, and only then the report of the gun that fired it. Your own rounds tear away downrange.

Breakwater's diesels answer the telegraph and the wash along the hull answers the speed actually made, so you can hear the ship gather way after a new order. They are modelled on the nine-cylinder double-acting two-strokes of the 1930s diesel cruisers, four to a shaft, and tuned against recordings made aboard ships under way: a cluster of steady low tones that labour as the engines are brought up, with no two engines quite in step. Over those tones is the exhaust from the funnel behind the bridge, a rougher sound that grows fastest when the engines are worked hard, and the engine-room telegraph rings its bell at every new order. A damaged engine has lost cylinders: it stumbles, and unburnt fuel goes off in its exhaust in fits of bangs. An engine that is knocked out is silent until the repair party has it turning again. At speed the wind rises over the bridge and the bow works into each swell. All of it gives way for a moment when a gun fires close by. At the title, and between legs, only the sea is heard.

The places you pass make their own sounds, each heard from where it stands and as late as its distance makes it. The sea breaks on every shore, kittiwakes call over the islands, a yard rivets until its siren goes or its gantry falls, ice splits, and now and then a glacier drops part of its front into the sea with a roar that carries a kilometre and more. The ship's own frames creak as she works, more often at speed. These are background: close to an island at half ahead they are plain, and at full ahead the engines and the wash cover most of them.

Each base's spotter is heard as well as seen: the drone of a two-bladed propeller on a nine-cylinder radial, tuned against recordings of biplanes flying past. Its sound reaches you from where the aeroplane was when it made it, so the note is sharp while it closes, drops by a third as it passes overhead and stays flat as it goes. Far off it comes and goes on the wind.

Enemy boats carry red markings. Every enemy hull is a row of modules, and a penetrating hit lands on the module it strikes. Stop the engines and the ship drifts. Her guns run on about 20 seconds of reserve power and then fall silent. A cruiser's engine sits under her after gun, and a battleship's two are amidships. Cruisers and battleships carry a repair party that restarts an engine in about 20 seconds, so keep hitting a ship you want stopped. Wrecking a module silences the gun standing on it. Further hits on a wrecked module carry half their weight into its neighbours, and a locked sight moves to the nearest sound module. A ship sinks with half her length wrecked. On a battleship only the armoured middle counts, and a hit that destroys a magazine under an end turret wrecks that module and blasts the ones beside it. A sinking ship sheds debris and sounds a short trumpet call on the bridge. Each wreck leaves a gold package. Your stern line hooks it within 96 metres and reels it aboard for 10 XP and repairs per store. Larger packages carry more stores. Breakwater starts with six modules. She carries a main gun on the second and fifth, the director and close guns on the third, and the engine and torpedo racks on the fourth. A hit lands on one module. Her engine sits low in the middle of the machinery space, clear of its bulkheads and of the side passages under the torpedo racks. A shell damages it only if its path carries it in there: through the side amidships, or down through the deck above. A round that bursts in the funnels or the bridge, in the side passages, or at the ends of the compartment hurts the machinery space but not the engine. Rams, ice and bombs still reach it. Breakwater’s engine can absorb two thirds of its compartment’s structure in damage before it fails (12 points on the stock hull). When every engine is out, reserve power gives her 15% of her normal speed at the ordered throttle. Operational main and secondary guns train at 30% speed, and main guns elevate at the same reduced rate. Reserve power lasts until an engine restarts. The crew mends each module slowly but cannot make good about a third of any damage at sea, and the repair party goes unasked to whatever is most urgent: a flooded module, then the engine, then a knocked-out fitting. A stopped engine is turning again in about 10 seconds. Stores from packages are new plate and go to the weakest module. Breakwater sinks when two thirds of her length is wrecked. That takes four compartments on the stock hull, five with one extension and six with both. The dock between legs makes good 40 hull points and restores the fittings on every module it leaves afloat, so a badly damaged ship sails on short of whole. A route with hull repairs adds 30. Her condition is reported as sound, scarred, battered or critical, and critical also means one more wrecked module would sink her. When that becomes true the klaxon sounds three times on the bridge, and again every twenty seconds for as long as it stays true. The BREAKWATER panel carries a damage-control strip with a cell for each module from stern to bow. A cell's bar shows the structure left in green, amber or red, a white tick marks how far the crew can mend it at sea, and a flooded module turns blue with a red border. The fittings are listed under their module: white when working, amber when damaged, red when out and grey when sound but without power. A gold border on a cell, or brackets round a fitting, mark where the repair party is working. Under the strip are the count of flooded modules against the number that sinks her, engine output, the party's task and the ship’s reserve-power status. The first level costs 30 XP, and each subsequent level costs 10 XP more. A level pauses combat while you choose a fitting, and each choice is answered with two rising notes, as is every work order spent in the dock. Unspent XP and installed fittings carry into the next leg.

Card hands come round in four themes, and a hand that runs out of its own cards is filled from the others. The first is ammunition: armor-piercing shells, a larger bore and high-velocity rounds. Once AP is fitted, piercing caps can improve its plate grade. A rebore steps up the calibre tree. Larger rounds tear a wider hole, take longer to load and train more slowly. Faster rounds shorten travel time, flatten the arc, carry their penetration grade farther and reach farther: 780 metres stock, about 900 with one card and 1,020 with three. The second hand is for the main battery: quick loaders, triple turrets and training gear. Fitting triples unlocks the quad turret card. Training gear speeds the training and elevation of every gunhouse by a quarter per card, up to four, which brings even a 15- or 18-inch mount back to about the stock pace. The third hand is for machinery and protection: turbine refits, armor plate and hardened systems. Each armor plate card adds half again to the strength of every stretch of side plating and three inches to its thickness. A hull carries two cards on her stock beam, four once widened to a cruiser's and six on a capital beam, and each step of beam needs a longer hull first. Sound plate turns aside any round no wider than it: the first two cards stop the enemy's secondaries and aircraft guns, then patrol boats and destroyers; the next two cruisers, then battleships and shore guns; the last two the Parliament's guns. The Regent's always get through. A turned round only scuffs the plate. Plate battered by heavier guns is thinner and lets smaller rounds in again, and plating that is gone lets through twice the hit. Hardened systems bring the engine up to five sixths and then all of its compartment's structure (15 and then 18 points on the stock hull), and add half again to the strength of every deck fitting per card, up to two. Every fourth hand is for the close-range and anti-aircraft guns, and each such hand leads with a different card: more secondaries add a wing gun to each beam, forward by the bridge and then aft by the launches, up to three a side; AA guns mount a pair of light anti-aircraft guns abaft the after funnel; twin secondaries give every wing gun a second barrel that fires with the first; heavier secondaries raise the damage of each hit from one to three; more AA guns add a pair on the bridge wings and then between the funnels; rangefinders let the crew open fire 40 metres farther out per card, up to 240 metres; heavier flak raises the damage of each AA burst from one to three; secondary loaders shorten their reload as quick loaders do for the main battery; and AA loaders do the same for the AA guns, up to three cards.

Cards are dealt face up. The coloured frame gives the category (gold for ammunition, red for the main battery, cyan for machinery, silver for protection, green for secondaries, blue-violet for anti-aircraft), the pips show how many ranks you hold and which one this card adds, and NEW marks a card that gives the ship something she could not do before. Each card shows the change before and after: green is a gain, red a cost and gold something new. Press 1 to 3 or click a card to fit it.

Hulls are solid. Each ship absorbs the closing speed in proportion to the other's share of their combined weight. Breakwater can sink a patrol boat struck on the beam at full ahead. A slower blow can cripple it or shove it aside. Contact below 2 m/s is a harmless bump. Ramming a ship of equal or greater weight costs the cruiser considerably more way and can leave her in critical condition.

The telegraph has three astern orders, stop, and four ahead orders. The highlighted notch is the engine order. The signed speed in knots shows the ship gathering or shedding way, including the time it takes to reverse.

## The sea

A long swell runs downwind with shorter waves on top of it, and every hull rides it according to her size. Breakwater pitches into a head sea and rolls when she lies beam-on. A patrol boat is thrown about, and a battleship barely nods. Buoys and supply crates bob on whatever passes under them. Gunnery is unaffected: shells, hits and collisions are still worked out on a flat sea.

| Waters | Weather |
| --- | --- |
| Helm trial | Light airs and a low ripple. |
| Lantern Bay | Sheltered water with a light swell. |
| Blackcap Reach | Heavy swell on an exposed coast under low cloud. |
| Iron Narrows | A moderate breeze under broken cloud. |
| Outer Road | A long open-water swell under a clearing sky. |
| Greywater Sound | Nearly flat under heavy overcast. |
| Pilot's Cut | A storm swell two to three metres high, with whitecaps and a dark sky. |

A ship under way pushes water aside at the bow and lets it fall astern. The waves that leave her travel at the speed their length gives them, so they spread in a V that grows longer and wider the faster she goes. The bow wave breaks white along her sides and trails astern as two lines of foam, which thin into lace within a few seconds. Between them she leaves a slick: a smooth, pale lane where the churned water has flattened the ripples, which takes about a quarter of a minute to roughen again. Raiders leave the same. A shell that misses throws out spreading rings, and a wreck draws the water in over itself as it goes down.

Disturbed water is kept for about 70 metres around Breakwater, and foam and slick for about 140. A raider beyond that has no wake.

## Coastal chart

The north-up mini-map follows the ship and shows 2.4 km across on a 200-metre grid. The white arrow is Breakwater, gold crosses mark stores, and red triangles show raiders out to 1,400 metres. Winged gold contacts mark spotter planes. The chart marks live bases A, B and C in coral and gives each cleared base a check mark. Matching flags above the gantries show distance. The top-centre bridge display keeps the mission clock, clearance, stars and all three base bearings in view while you pan or zoom. Each patrol sector has three bases along a mainland coast. Beacon islands, pine islets and rock stacks stand off the coast and provide cover. Gold bars mark supply freighters.

Every patrol is a passage along a craggy coast. Breakwater enters at one end and fights her way along the shore. Base A, with a single gun, stands in the first bay. Base B, with two twin turrets, is past halfway. The heavy yard, C, is always at the far end, where a last cape turns the coast out to sea. Each base stands on its own island at the head of its bay, with a channel behind it. A headland, or a glacier coming down to the sea, stands between one bay and the next, and its high ground hides the next base until you round it. Sea cliffs run along the whole shore, with crags standing out from them and snowfields and mountains behind. Every run charts fresh waters. The run's seed decides which way the coast runs, how deep its bays are and where its headlands stand, where the bases stand in their bays, and how the islands lie off the shore. Each sea keeps its character. The Iron Narrows runs between pairs of islands either side of the course. The Outer Road is a long, wide reach with scattered rocks and beacons. In Greywater Sound the course runs inside a broken chain of wooded islands. Pilot's Cut is a tight course hard under the crags, through crowded stacks. Every chart is surveyed before it is sailed. The survey checks for navigable channels between the islands and the shore. Each yard and factory needs a clear shot from water the cruiser can reach. Each slip must lead to open water, and each yard needs two clear freighter approaches. The bases must stand at least 360 metres apart and in order along the coast, and the course must pass each within range of its guns. The survey also checks that no point on the course is under the shore guns of two bases at once. A chart that fails is drawn again, and the best-scoring of the first three that pass is sailed. The helm trial keeps its fixed course. To sail a run's waters again, start the game with `SHIPVIVOR_SEED` set to the run seed from the log or a capture note. `cargo run -p shipvivor-encounter --example charts` draws sample charts of each sea to `work/charts/` with their survey figures, the current's streamlines and the ice.

The sectors lie in arctic water. Every sea has one or two glaciers three to six times the size of the other islands, under an ice cap whose glacier ends in a cliff at the sea. A glacier comes down to the coast between two bays where there is room, and otherwise stands offshore. The islands and the bases carry snow. A current sets along every coast, one way or the other. It is solved from the coast and the islands as a stream function. It follows the shore, parts round each island, runs faster through straits and round headlands, races off the last cape and slackens in the lee of an island. Pilot's Cut runs hardest, at up to about 1.5 knots in open water, and Greywater Sound is sluggish. No water anywhere runs faster than 1.25 m/s, so even the slowest flagship can make way against it. The current sets every hull, and the enemy gunners allow for it when they lead the cruiser. Wreckage drifts with it until the winch takes hold. Ice rides the current. Growlers are low lumps a few metres across. Bergs are domes, pinnacles or flat-topped slabs, with the odd slab 60 metres across. Ice stops shells and torpedoes and blocks the sight. Enough fire splits a berg, as does a torpedo for all but the biggest slabs, and ice the current pins against a shore breaks up there. A berg outweighs the cruiser many times over, so ramming one at speed stoves in her bow and stops her, while nosing into ice dead slow does no harm. Raiders steer round bergs. More ice drifts in from upstream as the current carries it away. The mini-map plots every berg big enough to hurt, and capture notes record the current and the nearest ice.

Every working harbour has a floatplane flying a circuit over its approaches. When the observer sights Breakwater within 320 metres, a **!** pops over the plane and the bridge calls its bearing. Offscreen alerts appear at the edge of the view. Tilt the bridge sight up to follow a high pass. A plane can see over low ground, lose you behind high ground and find you again. Destroying its home drydock calls the patrol off. [In-game view of the spotter and bridge alert](work/visualizations/spotter-patrol.jpg)

Base lookouts can sight Breakwater at 660 metres. Ship lookout range runs from 520 metres on a patrol boat to 900 metres on a capital ship. Planes and harbour lookouts send reports to enemies within 1,100 metres of the observer. Raiders head for the last reported position after losing contact. Shore guns can open fire at 320 metres.

Two patrol boats and two destroyers are already at sea on forward, beam and quarter approaches. Each port has a drydock, shore defences and its own steel stock. Look for the smoke on the horizon. Each working chimney sends up one thick plume that bends with the wind. A is a converted cannery with a timber derrick, one chimney and one single-gun turret. At B a blue conveyor links two tidal mills, with a twin turret at each end of the island. At C, two triple turrets and a quad guard three furnace halls and an ochre travelling crane. Its slings raise and lower a steel plate over a lighter in a flooded berth. Foam washes through the granite ledges along the approach. Shelling a factory knocks it out for the round and its plume disappears. The enemy's flag flies over the slip for as long as the dock is open.

Each working factory supplies three steel per minute on top of the dock's baseline of one. Each shore turret trains and reloads independently. The gunsight shows its remaining strength. A single has 6 points, a twin 12, a triple 18 and a quad 24. A freighter delivers six steel, repairs a surviving drydock and restores up to three points across its shore battery.

Each drydock holds an order until it can pay for that ship and the berth is clear. A patrol costs two steel, a destroyer four, a cruiser seven and a battleship twelve. Patrols launch first. Destroyer orders unlock at 1:05, cruisers at 2:40 and battleships at 5:10. Every enemy ship in a sector is either on patrol when you arrive or comes off a slip you can see. Two patrol boats and two destroyers lie on station along the course, each at one of three spots the run's seed picks. Everything after them launches from a standing yard. Supply freighters cast off from the nearest other yard still standing, never from the open sea, and sail unescorted. Once a gantry is down, that base launches nothing more, and a yard with no other yard left standing gets no more deliveries. The single-factory base builds up to destroyers, the two-factory base up to cruisers, and the heavy yard can build battleships. Reinforcements launch at least 275 metres away. Aim at the gantry to see what is building, its cost and the steel in store. Destroying the gantry closes the slip, shuts down the factories and silences every turret at that base for the rest of the leg. A factory that is knocked out crumbles in a run of crashes and falling brick. When the gantry goes, a fanfare sounds aboard at once, and the longer roar of the yard coming down reaches you a second or so later. A base that has you in sight or by report, or that is being hit, sounds its air-raid siren: two tones a minor third apart that wail as the motor is switched on and off. Like a gun's report it reaches you late, so a far base is seen to open fire before its siren is heard winding up. It keeps going for twelve seconds after the base loses you. When the gantry falls the siren comes down with it, and its note drops away to nothing in about four seconds, which is the sound of a base that is finished. An alerted base also sends up three parachute flares towards where it last saw you. They burst sixty metres up and drift down under red and white canopies for a quarter of a minute, and another three follow every sixteen seconds while the alarm lasts. A flagship sounds a deeper siren of her own for twenty seconds as she puts to sea.

## Enemy fleet

Breakwater flies a flag of your own. Choose **Customise** at the title and the camera closes on her masthead, where the flag itself is the preview. Pick a pattern from the tiles, each shown in your colours, then one of its slots and a colour for it. Each slot's icon shows where on the cloth its colour goes, and 1–6 choose a slot from the keyboard. A pattern with fewer slots keeps the colours of the ones it hides, so you can go back. **Random** (R) spins through flags and stops on a new pattern and colours. **Done** (Esc) returns to the title. The flag is kept between launches in `flag.txt` in Shipvivor's settings folder (`~/Library/Application Support/Shipvivor` on macOS), or in the file `SHIPVIVOR_FLAG_FILE` names. The enemy hoists new colours on every leg, and again whenever yours change, always chosen so that at least half its cloth shows colours Breakwater's flag does not. Enemy flags fly over each open slip, at every raider's masthead, from a staff at a patrol boat's stern and on the twin standards over the Brass Parliament's gantry, and they stay hoisted as a ship goes down. A flag carries two to four colours in horizontal bands, upright bands, diagonal slashes rising from the lower hoist, a canton over bands, quarters, four triangles meeting at the centre, or an X with one colour above and below it and another at either side. The patterns can take up to six colours, ready for patterns to come. On a drawn flag, colours that share an edge always differ enough in brightness to tell apart at a distance, so gold never touches white and red never touches green. A flag you pick may break that rule. `cargo run -p shipvivor-ensign --example sheet` paints sample flags, each followed by four rivals, to `work/flags/sheet.png`.

Patrol boats close quickly with one deck gun. Destroyers are faster than cruisers and lightly built, circle close in at about 80 metres, and fire a single gun fore and aft. Cruisers circle at medium range and fire from two twin mounts. Battleships turn slowly and bracket the ship with three triple mounts. Every enemy gun, ashore and afloat, leads Breakwater by her present course and speed, so holding a steady course at speed no longer keeps her safe: turning or changing speed after they fire is what makes their rounds fall wide. Knocking a turret off a cruiser or battleship silences that station. Destroyers carry two secondary mounts, cruisers four and battleships twelve. Your wing guns, one on each beam until you fit more, work both beams independently out to 160 metres, farther with rangefinders. Patrol boats, destroyers, cruisers and battleships drop packages worth one, two, three and seven stores respectively. Supply freighters carry four. The sight identifies the class under the crosshair, and larger red contacts on the chart mark heavier ships.

Breakwater starts with 4-inch HE shells. HE bursts on whatever it strikes and never glances off, but it opens only light plate, whatever the bore. Patrol boats and cruisers are built of light plate throughout. A battleship's belt is capital-grade plate, and its deck and gunhouses are reinforced. HE can knock out a battleship's exposed director and slow its salvos, and strip its secondaries, but not much else. The armor-piercing shells card adds AP, which opens reinforced plate from the start and capital plate with piercing caps or a larger bore. AP can still glance off, and against light plate it goes clean through for half damage, which the sight reports as an overpenetration. The board beside the main battery shows which round each barrel holds once AP is fitted. Hits on the director stay local. A glancing blow sends a streak skimming away with a metallic ring. A square hit that stops produces a short flash, a low knock and a dent. A penetration throws fragments, vents smoke and opens that patch to later rounds of the same or smaller bore. The sight reports the outcome. Armor grades and flight times are tuned for the game's compressed sea.

**The Brass Parliament** patrols every second-leg route. Look for the gold crown on the chart. Its twenty secondary mounts cover the flanks. Its ten gold-banded triple turrets fire in staggered groups. Each wing turret covers its own side, so clearing one flank gives you somewhere to sail. Aim at the gold roofs to break the mounts. Each one stands three stock rounds and falls to the fourth. A destroyed turret flies off and leaves a burning socket. The chart counts the remaining mounts. Her bridge, the tall tower amidships, takes hits even while her turrets guard the hull. Three stock rounds shoot it away. She then holds her course at about a third of her speed for 40 seconds, which gives you time to open the range or come round to her other flank. Her tag shows how long she has left without a helm. A bridge is lost only once. Once all ten turrets are gone the flagship drifts, its hull becomes vulnerable, and sinking it leaves a twelve-store package. You can still extract while it is afloat.

## Patrol and exits

Click an anchorage on the opening chart or press **1–3**. **Lantern Bay** gives you twelve minutes among wooded islands, with the longest interval between enemy launches. **The Iron Narrows** gives you ten minutes in winding channels. **Blackcap Reach** gives you nine minutes on an exposed coast where reinforcements arrive fastest. The patrol clock starts when you choose.

Tap **Tab** at sea to unroll the passage chart. The patrol pauses until you stow the chart with **Tab** or **Esc**. The pause menu also shows the chart. It plots the three exits from your chosen anchorage and their rendezvous at Crown Deep. Solid lines show available passages and dashed lines show locked alternatives. After drydock, click an open passage name or press **1–3** to sail it. Your chosen course appears in red on later charts. [View the passage chart](work/charts/passage-chart.png).

Taking the three-star **Pilot's Cut** brings you into enemy air cover. At **A**, a red-roofed seaplane hangar replaces the cannery. Lookouts can call a strafing run from there. The chart warns of the aircraft's bearing and range as it comes in. Turn after it lines up to let the burst walk across your wake. On this route the station at **Crown Deep** alternates bombers and strafers. Shell the hangar or destroy its drydock to stop further launches. The other two passages have reconnaissance flights only.

Once AA guns are fitted, the crew engages any enemy aircraft within 280 metres. Each gun fires a round fused to burst where the crew leads the aircraft to be. Bursts scatter about the aim, so a steady pass is hit often but not every time, and a plane caught in a burst takes its damage. A reconnaissance floatplane goes down after five hits and stays down for the rest of the leg, so its base loses its eyes in the air. A strafer or bomber takes eight, and its station needs 45 seconds to ready another. A stricken aircraft glides on as it falls and throws up a splash where it hits the water. A stock pair may take a few sorties to bring a strafer down. Against the full battery it rarely gets its burst off.

Each patrol awards one star for meeting the raider target, one for meeting the stores target, and one for destroying the first enemy drydock. The HUD tracks all three. In the separate helm trial, cross between the two buoys with gold lamps to advance the course. The trial HUD gives the bearing and distance to the active pair.

Destroy all three enemy shipyards to clear the sector immediately, even if enemy ships are still afloat. The clock freezes on the winning hit. A short clearance view shows the last dock collapsing before drydock or the final intercept briefing. Enter skips it. A damaged or empty yard can still take a delivery and recover. If time expires while Breakwater is afloat, she returns with the stars earned so far. The pause menu also offers early extraction after destroying one drydock or spending one minute at sea.

At the end of your first patrol, the dock crew repairs Breakwater and issues one work order, plus a second for clearing every base. XP choices earned on the final tick also become work orders. Two stars earn a five-metre reserve bay and a sector clearance earns an auxiliary engine. Both performance rewards are fitted before you customize the ship.

In the drydock the ship is the menu. Hover over her to see each section's name, and click one to select it. Its refits hang below it, grouped by what they change. Choosing one previews it on the ship, with its figures beside the list. Nothing is previewed until you choose, and the yard opens on the first bay that can take another turret.

The **HULL** panel at the bottom left shows her length and beam and holds the work on the whole hull: widening and armor plate. Widening costs one order and goes one step at a time, from 5.5 to 7.5 to 9.5 metres. Each step needs length first: 35 metres for 7.5 and 40 metres for 9.5, so she stays at least 4.2 beams long and her bow still parts the water at full speed. A widened hull draws no more water than before, because the yard adds frame weight in step with the waterplane. A two-star sector clearance brings her to the yard at 40 metres with two orders, which is enough to widen her twice. The bigger bores then come from field refits on the next leg.

**MODULES** beside it is the yard's stock of whole five-metre sections. Drag one onto the ship and drop it on the seam where it should go: a cyan slab marks the seam under the cursor. The new section is then tried in place with its figures, and **Fit** confirms it. The spanners in a tile's corner are its price in work orders, and they turn red when the yard cannot pay.

Sections earned on the first leg count toward these limits. The basin takes a hull of up to 60 metres. A new reserve bay is empty until you select it and fit a turret.

A turret bay lists the main battery bore, the barrels per turret, shells, loaders and training gear. These apply to every turret, whichever one you choose them from. Triple and quad turrets change the number of barrels in each turret, not the number of turrets. The bridge lists the secondary and AA guns, and an engine bay lists turbines and hardened systems. Returning to twin turrets is free if you want to pursue a larger bore. Figures below are the largest game calibres in inches for each barrel layout.

Each interior bay can be moved toward the bow or stern. Moves are free. Moving a turret relative to the bridge and machinery changes where its guns can bear. A bay is also where you add main turrets, up to four. **Add centre turret** fits one on an empty reserve bay, and **Add wing turrets** fits a port-and-starboard pair there. **Split into wing turrets** turns a centre turret into a pair, which adds a turret without a reserve bay. Each exchange costs one work order. A wing pair shares a single module, and each turret trains over its own side. Wing turrets take a smaller bore, and other centreline positions can keep their heavier guns. The bow, stern, machinery, director and final main turret are protected from exchanges that would remove them.

Click a refit or step through them with the arrow keys to preview it. **Enter** confirms. The last paid order closes the dock and opens the passage chart, so it asks for a second press of **Enter** or a second click. Choosing any other refit backs out. Finish any free moves before spending it. Fittings still follow the field card rules and cost work orders. The encounter clock stays paused throughout.

The second round starts with fresh bases and a fresh clock. Your ship, fittings, XP and run tally carry across. Clearing the second sector, surviving its deadline or extracting opens the final intercept in **Crown Deep**. Press **Enter** at the briefing to sail with your fitted ship. The crew restores up to 40 hull on the crossing.

**The Iron Regent** is a 168-metre dreadnought already at sea with a cruiser, two destroyers and a patrol boat. Her gold crown marks her bearing on the chart. Break her twelve gold turrets to expose the hull. Her heavy turrets flash and commit to a patch of water three seconds before firing. Changing course during the flash can spoil the shot. As one broadside falls silent, she turns to bring the surviving guns to bear. The remaining harbours can send reinforcements, so knocking out a factory can buy breathing room. Sinking the Regent wins the run. She escapes after fifteen minutes, and losing Breakwater ends the run on any leg.

Run `cargo test --locked` for the workspace or `cargo test --locked -p shipvivor-campaign` for a single simulation crate. The simulation crates run headlessly. The Bevy integration tests exercise the helm trial and the patrol from the title menu through upgrades, route selection, the Crown Deep intercept and the final report.

To hear the sound bank without playing, run `cargo run --locked -p shipvivor-acoustics --example audition -- tour.wav`. It writes twelve scenes to a WAV file and prints when each starts: the chime for an upgrade, the klaxon, the ship getting under way, an engine damaged and then knocked out and mended, the ship lying off an islet, a glacier and a working yard with a floatplane circling, the main battery firing among islands, a raider and a shore gun landing rounds close by, a base shelled with its siren going until it falls, a base's siren and a flagship's alone, a floatplane flying past, and every sound alone at three ranges. Building on Linux needs the ALSA development headers.

Every build logs each patrol run to `work/runs/<CODE>.log`, `cargo run --release` included. Build with `--no-default-features` to leave out the logbook and the ` capture key. The code is seven capital letters, shown in the window title. The log records each leg as it happens: ships sunk, hits landed and what became of them, hits taken and the damage to each section, wrecked bases, stores and stars, refits, routes and pauses. A pulse line sums up every 15 seconds of sailing. In Claude Code, `/recap` reads the runs of the latest sitting and talks them over, and `/recap <CODE>` reads one run.

A pale downward triangle marks a visible ship near the center of the view. Aim directly at a fitting to select that point. Pan onto open water to release the soft lock. X pins the selection and turns its triangle gold, even when you look astern. The chart panel shows the lock state, range and sighted fitting. In the Shift sight each numbered division is one target hull length, with half-length ticks between them. The lead cue estimates continued movement. In torpedo view the green fan shows the spread you will launch and a narrow white sector suggests a lead. The spread turns amber while that rack reloads. The run tally counts ship contacts from both gun batteries and torpedoes, including stopped rounds. Sunk ships and hits carry across the two legs.
