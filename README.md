# Shipvivor

Take the cruiser *Breakwater* through two coastal patrols and a final dreadnought intercept. Run `cargo run --locked` with Rust 1.97.1 or newer and a graphical desktop. From the title menu, choose **New run** to sail the Iron Narrows or **Helm trial** to practice the original buoy course.

## At the helm

| Control | Action |
| --- | --- |
| W / S | Each tap rings one notch on the engine telegraph. It holds the order. Patrols start at half ahead. |
| A / D | Hold the rudder to port / starboard. Release to centre it. |
| Mouse movement | Look in any direction, including while the guns track a pinned ship. In free-look a pale triangle picks out a ship near the crosshair. |
| Hold either Shift | Magnify the sight about 3× with finer mouse movement. Release to return to the cruising view. |
| C | Recenter the view astern, looking over the bow. |
| Left mouse | Click to fire one barrel. Double-click to fire every loaded barrel that bears on the sight. Hold to fire them one after another, 0.3 seconds apart, and keep firing each as it reloads. Aim lower for near water or raise the sight for a longer shot. |
| X | Pin the marked ship, or the nearest ship if there is no mark. The guns follow the pinned point while you look around. Press X again to release. |
| Hold right mouse and drag | Return the sight to the pinned ship and trim the aim left, right, up or down. Release to look around again. |
| T | Switch between guns and torpedoes. In torpedo mode, aim into either broadside sector and click to launch three. Each side reloads separately. |
| Q | Once armor-piercing shells are fitted, switch what the crew loads next between HE and AP. Barrels already loaded keep their round. |
| Space | Fire the main battery at the selected ship, falling back to the nearest contact when the sight is clear. Tap, double-tap and hold work like the left mouse button. The crew operates close-range secondaries automatically against ships and shore structures. |
| E | Extract when the route opens. |
| Esc | Pause a patrol, resume from pause, or return to the title from the trial or debrief. |
| Enter / 1–3 | Start a run / choose a field fitting or route. In drydock, the arrow keys or 1–9 preview a refit for the selected section and Enter confirms it. Menu choices are also clickable. |
| Click / [ ] in drydock | Select a section of the ship. Hovering over her names each section. The bow and stern hold the hull's own refits. **Esc** clears the preview. |
| R | Reset the helm trial. |

Each main mount has a labelled sight circle showing where that particular gun is pointing at the selected range. Pan with the trigger released to lay the guns in advance. A green circle is ready, amber is training, blue is reloading, and grey means the ship is in the way. Each fitted barrel has a pip beside the circle. An arrow at the edge of the view indicates an offscreen mount. The same markers follow the zoomed sight.

The **main battery** board above the helm instruments is a plan of Breakwater, bow up. Its turrets sit at their fitted positions and train with the real mounts, each barrel fills from the gunhouse out as it reloads, and a gold diamond on the rim marks where the sight is looking. Beside the plan, each fitted barrel has a load bar and reads READY, TRAINING, MASKED or its reload in tenths of a second. A gold arrow marks the barrel a click will fire next. The ready mounts take turns, and each mount cycles through its loaded barrels. The last line counts the loaded barrels on target. A click waits up to 0.4 seconds for a mount that is still training or about to load; the board reads STANDING BY until it fires.

Breakwater's twin gunhouses take their visual cue from the interwar light cruiser HMAS *Perth*. Breakwater starts with one twin mount at each end. The calibre tree runs through 4, 6, 8, 12, 15 and 18 inches. A wider hull supports larger gunhouses. Adding barrels to a turret takes up some of that room. Each main mount trains and loads independently. Stock reload takes 1.6 seconds per inch of that mount’s effective calibre, so light wing guns can reload while a heavy centreline gun is still loading. Quick Loaders improve both. A field refit offers the next bore or barrel count only when the ship can carry it. Each smaller shielded gun on the beams gives ships first priority. If its firing arc is clear of ships, the crew tracks the nearest visible enemy factory, shore gun or drydock within its fitted range. A bluff or another building can mask the target. The crew shifts fire when a structure goes down. Raider deck guns turn faster than the cruiser's main battery, while the shore turrets turn more slowly. These timings are tuned for the patrol's compressed scale. [Australian War Memorial photograph of Perth’s twin mounts](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C380081)

The main battery crosses 250 metres in about 0.7 seconds. A round rises and falls under game-scaled gravity, can pass over a nearer boat, and throws up a white column when it lands short or long. The shell body scales to the gun that fired it. A short tracer helps you follow small rounds at patrol distances.

Sound takes time to cross the water. You see a distant gun flash, or your own round land, before you hear it: about half a second for a raider firing at 85 metres and nearly three seconds for a splash at the limit of the sight. Distance takes the crack of a gun first and leaves the low roll. A pause or a fitting choice holds sound in the air along with the battle.

Every report also comes back off the sea, rolls away across open water, and returns a second or more later from the land and ice in earshot. The eight biggest and nearest answer: bases, islands and any iceberg ten metres or more across, with a glacier throwing back up to twice what a base island does. Sound from one side reaches the far ear a little late and a little duller, which is clearest on headphones. Your own guns are felt through the deck before their blast reaches the bridge, and for a moment afterwards everything else is dulled.

Shells are heard in flight. They travel faster than sound, so a round passing close arrives silently: first the crack of its shock, then the rush of its flight falling away in pitch, and only then the report of the gun that fired it. Your own rounds tear away downrange.

Breakwater's diesels answer the telegraph and the wash along the hull answers the speed actually made, so you can hear the ship gather way after a new order. They are modelled on the nine-cylinder double-acting two-strokes of the 1930s diesel cruisers, four to a shaft, and tuned against recordings made aboard ships under way: a cluster of steady low tones that labour as the engines are brought up, with no two engines quite in step. At speed the wind rises over the bridge and the bow works into each swell. All of it gives way for a moment when a gun fires close by. At the title, and between legs, only the sea is heard.

Enemy boats carry red markings. Every enemy hull is a row of modules, and a penetrating hit lands on the module it strikes. Stop the engines and the ship drifts. Her guns run on about 20 seconds of reserve power and then fall silent. A cruiser's engine sits under her after gun, and a battleship's two are amidships. Cruisers and battleships carry a repair party that restarts an engine in about 20 seconds, so keep hitting a ship you want stopped. Wrecking a module silences the gun standing on it. Further hits on a wrecked module carry half their weight into its neighbours, and a locked sight moves to the nearest sound module. A ship sinks with half her length wrecked. On a battleship only the armoured middle counts, and a hit that destroys a magazine under an end turret wrecks that module and blasts the ones beside it. A sinking ship sheds debris and sounds a short trumpet call on the bridge. Each wreck leaves a gold package. Your stern line hooks it within 96 metres and reels it aboard for 10 XP and repairs per store. Larger packages carry more stores. Breakwater starts with six modules. She carries a main gun on the second and fifth, the director and close guns on the third, and the engine and torpedo racks on the fourth. A hit lands on one module. Breakwater’s engine can absorb two thirds of its compartment’s structure in damage before it fails (12 points on the stock hull). When every engine is out, reserve power gives her 15% of her normal speed at the ordered throttle. Operational main and secondary guns train at 30% speed, and main guns elevate at the same reduced rate. Reserve power lasts until an engine restarts. The crew mends each module slowly but cannot make good about a third of any damage at sea, and the repair party goes unasked to whatever is most urgent: a flooded module, then the engine, then a knocked-out fitting. A stopped engine is turning again in about 10 seconds. Stores from packages are new plate and go to the weakest module. Breakwater sinks when two thirds of her length is wrecked. That takes four compartments on the stock hull, five with one extension and six with both. The dock between legs makes good 40 hull points and restores the fittings on every module it leaves afloat, so a badly damaged ship sails on short of whole. A route with hull repairs adds 30. Her condition is reported as sound, scarred, battered or critical, and critical also means one more wrecked module would sink her. The BREAKWATER panel carries a damage-control strip with a cell for each module from stern to bow. A cell's bar shows the structure left in green, amber or red, a white tick marks how far the crew can mend it at sea, and a flooded module turns blue with a red border. The fittings are listed under their module: white when working, amber when damaged, red when out and grey when sound but without power. A gold border on a cell, or brackets round a fitting, mark where the repair party is working. Under the strip are the count of flooded modules against the number that sinks her, engine output, the party's task and the ship’s reserve-power status. The first level costs 30 XP, and each subsequent level costs 10 XP more. A level pauses combat while you choose a fitting. Unspent XP and installed fittings carry into the next leg.

The first card hand offers armor-piercing shells, a larger bore and high-velocity rounds. Once AP is fitted, piercing caps can improve its plate grade. A rebore steps up the calibre tree. Larger rounds tear a wider hole and take longer to load. Faster rounds shorten travel time, flatten the arc and carry their penetration grade farther. Later hands also offer quick loaders, turbine refits and triple turrets. Fitting triples unlocks the quad turret card. Every fourth hand is for the close-range battery, and each such hand leads with a different card: more secondaries add a wing gun to each beam, forward by the bridge and then aft by the launches, up to three a side; twin secondaries give every wing gun a second barrel that fires with the first; heavier secondaries raise the damage of each hit from one to three; rangefinders let the crew open fire 40 metres farther out per card, up to 240 metres; and secondary loaders shorten their reload as quick loaders do for the main battery.

Cards are dealt face up. The coloured frame gives the category (gold for ammunition, red for the main battery, cyan for machinery, green for secondaries), the pips show how many ranks you hold and which one this card adds, and NEW marks a card that gives the ship something she could not do before. Each card shows the change before and after: green is a gain, red a cost and gold something new. Press 1 to 3 or click a card to fit it.

Hulls are solid. Each ship absorbs the closing speed in proportion to the other's share of their combined weight. Breakwater can sink a patrol boat struck on the beam at full ahead. A slower blow can cripple it or shove it aside. Contact below 2 m/s is a harmless bump. Ramming a ship of equal or greater weight costs the cruiser considerably more way and can leave her in critical condition.

The telegraph has three astern orders, stop, and four ahead orders. The highlighted notch is the engine order. The signed speed in knots shows the ship gathering or shedding way, including the time it takes to reverse.

## The sea

A long swell runs downwind with shorter waves on top of it, and every hull rides it according to her size. Breakwater pitches into a head sea and rolls when she lies beam-on. A patrol boat is thrown about, and a battleship barely nods. Buoys and supply crates bob on whatever passes under them. Gunnery is unaffected: shells, hits and collisions are still worked out on a flat sea.

| Waters | Weather |
| --- | --- |
| Helm trial | Light airs and a low ripple. |
| Iron Narrows | A moderate breeze under broken cloud. |
| Outer Road | A long open-water swell under a clearing sky. |
| Greywater Sound | Nearly flat under heavy overcast. |
| Pilot's Cut | A storm swell two to three metres high, with whitecaps and a dark sky. |

A ship under way pushes water aside at the bow and lets it fall astern. The waves that leave her travel at the speed their length gives them, so they spread in a V that grows longer and wider the faster she goes. The bow wave breaks white along her sides and trails astern as two lines of foam, which thin into lace within a few seconds. Between them she leaves a slick: a smooth, pale lane where the churned water has flattened the ripples, which takes about a quarter of a minute to roughen again. Raiders leave the same. A shell that misses throws out spreading rings, and a wreck draws the water in over itself as it goes down.

Disturbed water is kept for about 70 metres around Breakwater, and foam and slick for about 140. A raider beyond that has no wake.

## Coastal chart

The north-up mini-map follows the ship and shows 2.4 km across on a 200-metre grid. The white arrow is Breakwater, teal brackets mark the next buoy gate, gold crosses mark stores, and red triangles show raiders out to 1,400 metres. Winged gold contacts mark spotter planes. The chart marks live bases A, B and C in coral and gives each cleared base a check mark. Matching flags above the gantries show distance. The top-centre bridge display keeps the mission clock, clearance, stars and all three base bearings in view while you pan or zoom. Each patrol sector has five gates and three bases. Beacon islands, pine islets and rock stacks divide the fairways and provide cover. Gold bars mark supply freighters.

Every run charts fresh waters. The run's seed decides the shape of the buoy circuit and which way it turns, where the three bases stand and which leg each watches, and how the islands lie. Each sea keeps its character. The Iron Narrows runs between pairs of islands either side of the course. The Outer Road is wide open, with scattered rocks and beacons. Greywater Sound lies inside a broken ring of wooded islands. Pilot's Cut is a tight course through crowded stacks. Every chart is surveyed before it is sailed. Each gate must be reachable and clear to pass through. Each yard and factory needs a clear shot from water the cruiser can reach. Each slip must lead to open water, and each yard needs two clear freighter approaches. The bases must stand at least 360 metres apart and be spread round the circuit, and no gate may lie under two shore guns. A chart that fails is drawn again, and the best-scoring of the first three that pass is sailed. The helm trial keeps its fixed course. To sail a run's waters again, start the game with `SHIPVIVOR_SEED` set to the run seed from the log or a capture note. `cargo run -p shipvivor-encounter --example charts` draws sample charts of each sea to `work/charts/` with their survey figures, the current's streamlines and the ice.

The sectors lie in arctic water. Most seas have one or two glaciers: islands three to six times the size of the others, under an ice cap whose glacier ends in a cliff at the sea. The other islands and the bases carry snow. A current sets across every sector. It is solved from the islands as a stream function, so it parts round each island, runs faster through straits and past headlands, and slackens in the lee. Pilot's Cut runs hardest, at up to about 1.5 knots in open water, and Greywater Sound is sluggish. No water anywhere runs faster than 1.25 m/s, so even the slowest flagship can make way against it. The current sets every hull, and the enemy gunners allow for it when they lead the cruiser. Wreckage drifts with it until the winch takes hold. Ice rides the current. Growlers are low lumps a few metres across. Bergs are domes, pinnacles or flat-topped slabs, with the odd slab 60 metres across. Ice stops shells and torpedoes and blocks the sight. Enough fire splits a berg, as does a torpedo for all but the biggest slabs, and ice the current pins against a shore breaks up there. A berg outweighs the cruiser many times over, so ramming one at speed stoves in her bow and stops her, while nosing into ice dead slow does no harm. Raiders steer round bergs. More ice drifts in from upstream as the current carries it away. The mini-map plots every berg big enough to hurt, and capture notes record the current and the nearest ice.

Every working harbour has a floatplane flying a circuit over its approaches. When the observer sights Breakwater within 320 metres, a **!** pops over the plane and the bridge calls its bearing. Offscreen alerts appear at the edge of the view. Tilt the bridge sight up to follow a high pass. A plane can see over low ground, lose you behind high ground and find you again. Destroying its home drydock calls the patrol off. [In-game view of the spotter and bridge alert](work/visualizations/spotter-patrol.jpg)

Base lookouts can sight Breakwater at 660 metres. Ship lookout range runs from 520 metres on a patrol boat to 900 metres on a capital ship. Planes and harbour lookouts send reports to enemies within 1,100 metres of the observer. Raiders head for the last reported position after losing contact. Shore guns can open fire at 320 metres.

Two patrol boats and two destroyers are already at sea on forward, beam and quarter approaches. Each port has a drydock, shore defences and its own steel stock. Look for the smoke on the horizon. Each working chimney sends up one thick plume that bends with the wind. A is a converted cannery with a timber derrick, one chimney and one single-gun turret. At B a blue conveyor links two tidal mills, with a twin turret at each end of the island. At C, two triple turrets and a quad guard three furnace halls and an ochre travelling crane. Its slings raise and lower a steel plate over a lighter in a flooded berth. Foam washes through the granite ledges along the approach. Shelling a factory knocks it out for the round and its plume disappears. The enemy's flag flies over the slip for as long as the dock is open.

Each working factory supplies three steel per minute on top of the dock's baseline of one. Each shore turret trains and reloads independently. The gunsight shows its remaining strength. A single has 6 points, a twin 12, a triple 18 and a quad 24. A freighter delivers six steel, repairs a surviving drydock and restores up to three points across its shore battery.

Each drydock holds an order until it can pay for that ship and the berth is clear. A patrol costs two steel, a destroyer four, a cruiser seven and a battleship twelve. Patrols launch first. Destroyer orders unlock at 1:05, cruisers at 2:40 and battleships at 5:10. Convoys bound for ports with operating factories can bring an escort in from another approach. The single-factory base builds up to destroyers, the two-factory base up to cruisers, and the heavy yard can build battleships. Reinforcements launch at least 275 metres away. Aim at the gantry to see what is building, its cost and the steel in store. Destroying the gantry closes the slip, shuts down the factories and silences every turret at that base for the rest of the leg. A factory that is knocked out crumbles in a run of crashes and falling brick. When the gantry goes, a fanfare sounds aboard at once, and the longer roar of the yard coming down reaches you a second or so later. A base that has you in sight or by report, or that is being hit, sounds its air-raid siren: two tones a minor third apart that wail as the motor is switched on and off. Like a gun's report it reaches you late, so a far base is seen to open fire before its siren is heard winding up. It keeps going for twelve seconds after the base loses you, and when the gantry falls it loses its power and runs down. A flagship sounds a deeper siren of her own for twenty seconds as she puts to sea.

## Enemy fleet

Each run sails under its own flag. Breakwater's colours come from the run's seed, and the title already flies the flag your next run sails under. The enemy hoists new colours on every leg, always chosen so that at least half its cloth shows colours Breakwater's flag does not. Enemy flags fly over each open slip, at every raider's masthead, from a staff at a patrol boat's stern and on the twin standards over the Brass Parliament's gantry, and they stay hoisted as a ship goes down. A flag carries two to four colours in horizontal bands, upright bands, quarters, a canton over bands, or four triangles meeting at the centre. Colours that share an edge always differ enough in brightness to tell apart at a distance, so gold never touches white and red never touches green. The log names both flags when a leg begins. `cargo run -p shipvivor-ensign --example sheet` paints sample flags, each followed by four rivals, to `work/flags/sheet.png`.

Patrol boats close quickly with one deck gun. Destroyers are faster than cruisers and lightly built, circle close in at about 80 metres, and fire a single gun fore and aft. Cruisers circle at medium range and fire from two twin mounts. Battleships turn slowly and bracket the ship with three triple mounts. Every enemy gun, ashore and afloat, leads Breakwater by her present course and speed, so holding a steady course at speed no longer keeps her safe: turning or changing speed after they fire is what makes their rounds fall wide. Knocking a turret off a cruiser or battleship silences that station. Destroyers carry two secondary mounts, cruisers four and battleships twelve. Your wing guns, one on each beam until you fit more, work both beams independently out to 160 metres, farther with rangefinders. Patrol boats, destroyers, cruisers and battleships drop packages worth one, two, three and seven stores respectively. Supply freighters carry four. The sight identifies the class under the crosshair, and larger red contacts on the chart mark heavier ships.

Breakwater starts with 4-inch HE shells. HE bursts on whatever it strikes and never glances off, but it opens only light plate, whatever the bore. Patrol boats and cruisers are built of light plate throughout. A battleship's belt is capital-grade plate, and its deck and gunhouses are reinforced. HE can knock out a battleship's exposed director and slow its salvos, and strip its secondaries, but not much else. The armor-piercing shells card adds AP, which opens reinforced plate from the start and capital plate with piercing caps or a larger bore. AP can still glance off, and against light plate it goes clean through for half damage, which the sight reports as an overpenetration. The board beside the main battery shows which round each barrel holds once AP is fitted. Hits on the director stay local. A glancing blow sends a streak skimming away with a metallic ring. A square hit that stops produces a short flash, a low knock and a dent. A penetration throws fragments, vents smoke and opens that patch to later rounds of the same or smaller bore. The sight reports the outcome. Armor grades and flight times are tuned for the game's compressed sea.

**The Brass Parliament** patrols every second-leg route. Look for the gold crown on the chart. Its twenty secondary mounts cover the flanks. Its ten gold-banded triple turrets fire in staggered groups. Each wing turret covers its own side, so clearing one flank gives you somewhere to sail. Aim at the gold roofs to break the mounts. A destroyed turret flies off and leaves a burning socket. The chart counts the remaining mounts. Once all ten are gone the flagship drifts, its hull becomes vulnerable, and sinking it leaves a twelve-store package. You can still extract while it is afloat.

## Patrol and exits

Each leg awards one star per completed goal. The HUD tracks raiders sunk, stores recovered and buoy gates crossed. Cross between the two buoys with gold lamps to advance the course. The HUD gives the bearing and distance to the active pair.

Destroy all three enemy shipyards to clear the sector immediately, even if enemy ships are still afloat. The clock freezes on the winning hit. A short clearance view shows the last dock collapsing before drydock or the final intercept briefing. Enter skips it. A damaged or empty yard can still take a delivery and recover. If time expires while Breakwater is afloat, she returns with the stars earned so far. The Iron Narrows gives you ten minutes. **E** also allows early extraction after two gate crossings or one minute at sea.

At the end of the Iron Narrows, the dock crew repairs Breakwater and issues one work order, plus a second for clearing every base. XP choices earned on the final tick also become work orders. Two stars earn a five-metre reserve bay and a sector clearance earns an auxiliary engine. Both performance rewards are fitted before you customize the ship.

In the drydock the ship is the menu. Hover over her to see each section's name, and click one to select it. Its refits hang below it, grouped by what they change. Choosing one previews it on the ship, with its figures beside the list. Nothing is previewed until you choose, and the yard opens on the first bay that can take another turret.

Click the bow or stern for the hull's own work: length extensions and a beam refit. A reserve bay costs one work order and an auxiliary engine costs two. A new reserve bay is empty until you select it and fit a turret. Each kind of extension fits twice, including sections earned on the first leg. Widening costs one order. A two-star sector clearance lengthens the hull to 40 metres and qualifies it for the capital beam in one refit. This leaves enough orders to widen and fit 18-inch twins before the second leg.

A turret bay lists the main battery bore, the barrels per turret, shells and loaders. These apply to every turret, whichever one you choose them from. Triple and quad turrets change the number of barrels in each turret, not the number of turrets. The bridge lists the secondary guns, and an engine bay lists turbines. Returning to twin turrets is free if you want to pursue a larger bore. Figures below are the largest game calibres in inches for each barrel layout.

| Beam | Centre twin / triple / quad | Wing twin / triple / quad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Escort · 5.5 m | 6 / 4 / 4 | 4 / — / — |
| Cruiser · 9 m | 12 / 8 / 6 | 6 / 4 / 4 |
| Capital · 14 m | 18 / 15 / 12 | 8 / 8 / 6 |

Each interior bay can be moved toward the bow or stern. Moves are free. Moving a turret relative to the bridge and machinery changes where its guns can bear. A bay is also where you add main turrets, up to four. **Add centre turret** fits one on an empty reserve bay, and **Add wing turrets** fits a port-and-starboard pair there. **Split into wing turrets** turns a centre turret into a pair, which adds a turret without a reserve bay. Each exchange costs one work order. A wing pair shares a single module, and each turret trains over its own side. Wing turrets take a smaller bore, and other centreline positions can keep their heavier guns. The bow, stern, machinery, director and final main turret are protected from exchanges that would remove them.

Click a refit or step through them with the arrow keys to preview it. **Enter** confirms. The last paid order closes the dock and opens the passage chart, so it asks for a second press of **Enter** or a second click. Choosing any other refit backs out. Finish any free moves before spending it. Fittings still follow the field card rules and cost work orders. The encounter clock stays paused throughout.

| Exit from the Iron Narrows | Required stars | Second leg |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer Road | 0 | A 12-minute patrol on a wide channel. |
| Greywater Sound | 2 | A 12-minute winding course with hull repairs on arrival. |
| Pilot's Cut | 3 | A 15-minute tight course with a free fitting and faster enemy waves. |

The second round starts with fresh bases and a fresh clock. Your ship, fittings, XP and run tally carry across. Clearing the second sector, surviving its deadline or extracting opens the final intercept in **Crown Deep**. Press **Enter** at the briefing to sail with your fitted ship. The crew restores up to 40 hull on the crossing.

**The Iron Regent** is a 168-metre dreadnought already at sea with a cruiser, two destroyers and a patrol boat. Her gold crown marks her bearing on the chart. Break her twelve gold turrets to expose the hull. Her heavy turrets flash and commit to a patch of water three seconds before firing. Changing course during the flash can spoil the shot. As one broadside falls silent, she turns to bring the surviving guns to bear. The remaining harbours can send reinforcements, so knocking out a factory can buy breathing room. Sinking the Regent wins the run. She escapes after fifteen minutes, and losing Breakwater ends the run on any leg.

A pale downward triangle marks a visible ship near the center of the view. Aim directly at a fitting to select that point. Pan onto open water to release the soft lock. X pins the selection and turns its triangle gold, even when you look astern. The chart panel shows the lock state, range and sighted fitting. In the Shift sight each numbered division is one target hull length, with half-length ticks between them. The lead cue estimates continued movement. In torpedo view the green fan shows the spread you will launch and a narrow white sector suggests a lead. The spread turns amber while that rack reloads. The run tally counts ship contacts from both gun batteries and torpedoes, including stopped rounds. Sunk ships and hits carry across the two legs.
