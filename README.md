# Shipvivor

Take the cruiser *Breakwater* up a chain of seven rounds: six short patrols, choosing the next passage from each yard, and a final dreadnought intercept. A patrol runs five or six minutes, and a clean sweep of its bases takes about four. Run `cargo run --locked` with Rust 1.97.1 or newer and a graphical desktop. The window opens maximized on the screen it was last on, which is kept in `window.txt` in Shipvivor's settings folder, or in the file `SHIPVIVOR_WINDOW_FILE` names. From the title menu, choose **New run** to pick your first patrol on the chart, **Helm trial** to practice the original buoy course, or **Customise** to pick the flag Breakwater flies.

## At the helm

Breakwater’s captain stands on the forecastle as the ship cruises past the title camera. At sea, her portrait sits beside the telegraph. She flinches when the hull takes a hit or a spotter raises the alarm, braces through hard turns and engine trouble, and grins when an enemy ship goes down.

Each main mount has a labelled sight circle showing where that particular gun is pointing at the selected range. Pan with the trigger released to lay the guns in advance. A green circle is ready, amber is training, blue is reloading, and grey means the ship is in the way. Each fitted barrel has a pip beside the circle. An arrow at the edge of the view indicates an offscreen mount. The same markers follow the zoomed sight. Beside the sight a rangefinder reads the distance from the ship to whatever lies under the crosshair, whether sea, shore, a base, a gun or a ship, and how many seconds the slowest main round in reach takes to get there. Past every gun's reach it reads the distance alone, in grey, and it goes blank when the sight is on open sky. It is hidden in torpedo view.

A faint line traces the flight of a round from each main mount that can bear, rising and falling to the sight and stopping where the round would strike rock, ice, a building or a ship. The mount that fires next draws a little stronger. A main battery round carries 780 metres, and faster rounds carry farther in step with their muzzle speed. As the sight nears that limit, a dotted amber line fades in on the water at the guns' greatest reach either side of the sight's bearing. Aim past it and the lines show the rounds coming down on the dotted line, short of the sight. Both are hidden in torpedo view.

The **main battery** board above the helm instruments is a plan of Breakwater, bow up. Its turrets sit at their fitted positions and train with the real mounts, each barrel fills from the gunhouse out as it reloads, and a gold diamond on the rim marks where the sight is looking. Beside the plan, each fitted barrel has a load bar and reads READY, TRAINING, MASKED or its reload in tenths of a second. A gold arrow marks the barrel a click will fire next. The ready mounts take turns, and each mount cycles through its loaded barrels. The last line counts the trigger's loaded barrels on target. A click waits up to 0.4 seconds for a mount that is still training or about to load; the board reads STANDING BY until it fires.

Each turret is numbered on the plan from the bow, port before starboard abreast, and its block on the board is headed by its number and who has it. A digit key picks a turret out, ringing it in gold on the plan and boxing its number. **Z** then steps it through three controls, or steps every turret when none is picked out:

- **MANUAL**, the stock setting: the sight lays it and only the trigger fires it.
- **SCOPE**: it is yours while you hold Shift and it can bear on the sight. When the scope is down, or the sight is out of its arc, the crew has it as on AUTO, and the board reads SCOPE (AUTO).
- **AUTO**: the crew picks its own target and fires each barrel as it loads, 0.3 seconds apart. It takes the nearest enemy ship it can see in its arc and reach that has found Breakwater or come within 160 metres, leading her by her way, and failing one, the nearest gun it can see at a base that is awake. It leaves sleeping ships and bases, drydocks and factories to you. With nothing to fight, it follows the sight without firing.

A turret the crew has shows its number and control in lilac, its sight ring rides on the crew's target, and it draws no flight line. The trigger never fires it, and when the crew has every turret the last line reads ALL AUTO. Controls are kept by turret number from leg to leg.

Breakwater's twin gunhouses take their visual cue from the interwar light cruiser HMAS *Perth*. Breakwater starts with one single-gun turret at each end, and the yard gives each turret its barrels and bore on its own. The calibre tree runs through 4, 6, 8, 12, 15 and 18 inches. A wider hull supports larger gunhouses. Adding barrels to a turret takes up some of that room. Each main mount trains and loads independently. A heavier gunhouse trains and elevates more slowly: 18 degrees a second at 4 inches, about 13 at 8 inches and 8.5 at 18 inches. Stock reload takes 1.6 seconds per inch of that mount’s effective calibre, so light wing guns can reload while a heavy centreline gun is still loading. Quick Loaders improve both. The yard offers the next bore or barrel count only when the ship can carry it. Each smaller shielded gun on the bow and the beams gives ships first priority. If its firing arc is clear of ships, the crew tracks the nearest visible enemy factory, shore gun or drydock within its fitted range. A bluff or another building can mask the target. The crew shifts fire when a structure goes down. Raider deck guns turn faster than the cruiser's main battery, while the shore turrets turn more slowly. These timings are tuned for the patrol's compressed scale. [Australian War Memorial photograph of Perth’s twin mounts](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C380081)

The main battery crosses 250 metres in about 0.7 seconds. A round rises and falls under game-scaled gravity, can pass over a nearer boat, and throws up a white column when it lands short or long. The shell body scales to the gun that fired it. A short tracer helps you follow small rounds at patrol distances.

Sound takes time to cross the water. You see a distant gun flash, or your own round land, before you hear it: about half a second for a raider firing at 85 metres and nearly three seconds for a splash at the limit of the sight. Distance takes the crack of a gun first and leaves the low roll. A pause or a fitting choice holds sound in the air along with the battle.

Every report also comes back off the sea, rolls away across open water, and returns a second or more later from the land and ice in earshot. The eight biggest and nearest answer: bases, islands and any iceberg ten metres or more across, with a glacier throwing back up to twice what a base island does. Sound from one side reaches the far ear a little late and a little duller, which is clearest on headphones. Your own guns are felt through the deck before their blast reaches the bridge, and for a moment afterwards everything else is dulled.

Shells are heard in flight. They travel faster than sound, so a round passing close arrives silently: first the crack of its shock, then the rush of its flight falling away in pitch, and only then the report of the gun that fired it. Your own rounds tear away downrange. A heavy battery's shell is the one exception. It goes up on a long lob that is slower than sound, so its deep report reaches the bridge first, then its rush comes down out of the sky, rising in pitch as it nears, and then the column goes up.

Breakwater's diesels answer the telegraph and the wash along the hull answers the speed actually made, so you can hear the ship gather way after a new order. They are modelled on the nine-cylinder double-acting two-strokes of the 1930s diesel cruisers, four to a shaft, and tuned against recordings made aboard ships under way: a cluster of steady low tones that labour as the engines are brought up, with no two engines quite in step. Over those tones is the exhaust from the funnel behind the bridge, a rougher sound that grows fastest when the engines are worked hard, and the engine-room telegraph rings its bell at every new order. A damaged engine has lost cylinders: it stumbles, and unburnt fuel goes off in its exhaust in fits of bangs. An engine that is knocked out is silent until the repair party has it turning again. At speed the wind rises over the bridge and the bow works into each swell. All of it gives way for a moment when a gun fires close by. At the title, and between legs, only the sea is heard.

The places you pass make their own sounds, each heard from where it stands and as late as its distance makes it. The sea breaks on every shore, kittiwakes call over the islands, a yard rivets until its siren goes or its gantry falls, ice splits, and now and then a glacier drops part of its front into the sea with a roar that carries a kilometre and more. The ship's own frames creak as she works, more often at speed. These are background: close to an island at half ahead they are plain, and at full ahead the engines and the wash cover most of them.

Each base's spotter is heard as well as seen: the drone of a two-bladed propeller on a nine-cylinder radial, tuned against recordings of biplanes flying past. Its sound reaches you from where the aeroplane was when it made it, so the note is sharp while it closes, drops by a third as it passes overhead and stays flat as it goes. Far off it comes and goes on the wind. The harriers fly the same engine and are heard the same way, up to eight at once, the nearest first.

Enemy boats carry red markings. Every enemy hull is a row of modules, and a penetrating hit lands on the module it strikes. Stop the engines and the ship drifts. Her guns run on about 20 seconds of reserve power and then fall silent. A cruiser's engine sits under her after gun, and a battleship's two are amidships. Cruisers and battleships carry a repair party that restarts an engine in about 20 seconds, so keep hitting a ship you want stopped. Wrecking a module silences the gun standing on it. Further hits on a wrecked module carry half their weight into its neighbours, and a locked sight moves to the nearest sound module. A ship sinks with half her length wrecked. On a battleship only the armoured middle counts, and a hit that destroys a magazine under an end turret wrecks that module and blasts the ones beside it. A sinking ship sheds debris and sounds a short trumpet call on the bridge. Each wreck leaves a gold package. Your stern line hooks it within 96 metres and reels it aboard for 10 XP and repairs per store. Larger packages carry more stores. Breakwater starts with six modules. She carries a main gun on the second and fifth, the director and close guns on the third, and the engine and torpedo racks on the fourth, with a pair of close guns on the forecastle. A hit lands on one module. Her engine sits low in the middle of the machinery space, clear of its bulkheads and of the side passages under the torpedo racks. A shell damages it only if its path carries it in there: through the side amidships, or down through the deck above. A round that bursts in the funnels or the bridge, in the side passages, or at the ends of the compartment hurts the machinery space but not the engine. Rams, ice and bombs still reach it. Breakwater’s engine can absorb two thirds of its compartment’s structure in damage before it fails (12 points on the stock hull). When every engine is out, reserve power gives her 15% of her normal speed at the ordered throttle. Operational main and secondary guns train at 30% speed, and main guns elevate at the same reduced rate. Reserve power lasts until an engine restarts. The crew mends each module slowly but cannot make good about a third of any damage at sea, and the repair party goes unasked to whatever is most urgent: a flooded module, then the engine, then one filling faster than the crew can pump it, then a knocked-out fitting. A stopped engine is turning again in about 10 seconds. Stores from packages are new plate and go to the weakest module. A battered module of Breakwater's leaks, slowly at first and faster the more of its structure is gone. A module holed to nothing floods at once. The crew's pumps clear every module at a steady rate, enough to keep one dry down to about a third of its structure, and the repair party pumps harder wherever it is sent. Enemy ships do not leak: theirs flood only when holed to nothing. A module that fills floods and is out of action, and it is back in service once pumped down to half full, which means patching it until it leaks slower than the pumps. Stores patch the hole but do not bail, so a flooded module drains over the next few seconds. Water aboard drags on her speed as a flooded module does, by how full each module is, and the dock pumps her dry. Breakwater sinks when two thirds of her length is wrecked. That takes four compartments on the stock hull, five with one extension and six with both. The dock between legs makes good 40 hull points and restores the fittings on every module it leaves afloat, so a badly damaged ship sails on short of whole. A route with hull repairs adds 30. Her condition is reported as sound, scarred, battered or critical, and critical also means one more wrecked module would sink her. When that becomes true the klaxon sounds three times on the bridge, and again every twenty seconds for as long as it stays true. The BREAKWATER panel carries a damage-control strip with a cell for each module from stern to bow. A cell's bar shows the structure left in green, amber or red, a white tick marks how far the crew can mend it at sea, water rises in pale blue from the bottom of the cell as the module fills, and a flooded module turns blue with a red border. The fittings are listed under their module: white when working, amber when damaged, red when out and grey when sound but without power. A gold border on a cell, or brackets round a fitting, mark where the repair party is working. Under the strip are the count of flooded modules against the number that sinks her, engine output, the party's task and the ship’s reserve-power status. The first level costs 30 XP, and each subsequent level costs 10 XP more. A level pauses combat while you choose a fitting, and each choice is answered with two rising notes, as is every refit paid for in the dock. Unspent XP and installed fittings carry into the next leg.

Each level deals a hand of three different cards, drawn from the run's seed among the cards the ship can still take. A common card comes up six times as often as a rare one, and an uncommon card three times as often. Quick loaders, shell velocity, training gear, turbine refits, secondary loaders, rangefinders, heavier secondaries, more AA guns, heavier flak, AA loaders and bilge pumps are common. Armor-piercing shells, piercing caps, armor plate, hardened systems and more secondaries are uncommon. Twin secondaries are rare. A hand stands until you choose from it, and the same seed and the same choices deal the same hands. The bore and the barrels per turret are never dealt: the yard sells them. Once AP is fitted, piercing caps can improve its plate grade. The yard's rebore steps up the calibre tree, and larger rounds tear a wider hole, take longer to load and train more slowly. Faster rounds shorten travel time, flatten the arc, carry their penetration grade farther and reach farther: 780 metres stock, about 900 with one card and 1,020 with three. Training gear speeds the training and elevation of every gunhouse by a quarter per card, up to four, which brings even a 15- or 18-inch mount back to about the stock pace. Turbine refits add speed. Each armor plate card adds half again to the strength of every stretch of side plating and three inches to its thickness. An escort's hull carries the first third of the cards, a heavy cruiser's two thirds and a battleship's all of them, and each class of hull needs a longer hull first. Sound plate turns aside any round no wider than it: the first two cards stop the enemy's secondaries and aircraft guns, then patrol boats and destroyers; the next two cruisers, then battleships and shore guns; the last two the Parliament's guns and the heavy batteries. The Regent's always get through. A turned round only scuffs the plate. Plate battered by heavier guns is thinner and lets smaller rounds in again, and plating that is gone lets through twice the hit. Hardened systems bring the engine up to five sixths and then all of its compartment's structure (15 and then 18 points on the stock hull), and add half again to the strength of every deck fitting per card, up to two. Bilge pumps add half again to the pumping of the crew and of the repair party per card, up to three, so a holed module stays dry with less of its structure left: down to 31% of it on the stock pumps, then 21%, 13% and 6%. The card shows both figures. For the close-range and anti-aircraft guns, more secondaries add a wing gun to each beam, forward by the bridge and then aft by the launches, up to four a side with the bow gun; twin secondaries give every wing gun a second barrel that fires with the first; heavier secondaries raise the damage of each hit from one to three; more AA guns add a pair abaft the after funnel and then between the funnels, up to six guns; rangefinders let the crew open fire 40 metres farther out per card, up to 240 metres, and each raises a stepped tier on the bridge roof with the spotting top, its rangefinder arms out to either side, riding on the highest; heavier flak raises the damage of each AA burst from one to three; secondary loaders shorten their reload as quick loaders do for the main battery; and AA loaders do the same for the AA guns, up to three cards. On a battleship's hull the bridge also offers **secondary turrets**, which the yard alone fits: every wing gun is rebuilt as a four-inch gunhouse like the main battery's first turrets, twin-barrelled once twinned, still laid and fired by the crew on its own. Each hit does two more damage and reaches 100 metres farther, and the heavier round loads half again as slowly. A heavy battleship's hull takes a second rank, **six-inch** gunhouses: five more damage than a shielded mount, 180 metres more reach, and two and a quarter times its loading. A gunhouse fires a round of its own bore, so a four- or six-inch secondary pierces plate that a shielded mount's light round only scuffs. On a lighter hull the tile is padlocked and the hull tile strobes.

Cards are dealt face up. The coloured frame gives the category (gold for ammunition, red for the main battery, cyan for machinery, silver for protection, green for secondaries, blue-violet for anti-aircraft), the pips show how many ranks you hold and which one this card adds, NEW marks a card that gives the ship something she could not do before, and the gems in the art window's top left corner give its rarity: one grey gem for a common card, two silver for an uncommon one and three glowing gold for a rare one. Each card shows the change before and after: green is a gain, red a cost and gold something new. Press 1 to 3 or click a card to fit it.

Hulls are solid. Each ship absorbs the closing speed in proportion to the other's share of their combined weight. Breakwater can sink a patrol boat struck on the beam at full ahead. A slower blow can cripple it or shove it aside. Contact below 2 m/s is a harmless bump. Ramming a ship of equal or greater weight costs the cruiser considerably more way and can leave her in critical condition.

The telegraph has three astern orders, stop, and four ahead orders. The highlighted notch is the engine order. The signed speed in knots shows the ship gathering or shedding way, including the time it takes to reverse.

## The sea

A long swell runs downwind with shorter waves on top of it, and every hull rides it according to her size. Breakwater pitches into a head sea and rolls when she lies beam-on. A patrol boat is thrown about, and a battleship barely nods. Buoys and supply crates bob on whatever passes under them. Gunnery is unaffected: shells, hits and collisions are still worked out on a flat sea.

A ship under way pushes water aside at the bow and lets it fall astern. The waves that leave her travel at the speed their length gives them, so they spread in a V that grows longer and wider the faster she goes. The bow wave breaks white along her sides and trails astern as two lines of foam, which thin into lace within a few seconds. Between them she leaves a slick: a smooth, pale lane where the churned water has flattened the ripples, which takes about a quarter of a minute to roughen again. Raiders leave the same. A shell that misses throws out spreading rings, and a wreck draws the water in over itself as it goes down.

Disturbed water is kept for about 70 metres around Breakwater, and foam and slick for about 140. A raider beyond that has no wake.

## Coastal chart

The north-up mini-map follows the ship and shows 2.4 km across on a 200-metre grid. The white arrow is Breakwater, gold crosses mark stores, and red triangles show raiders out to 1,400 metres. Winged gold contacts mark spotter planes, and small red chevrons mark harriers. The chart marks each base it shows in coral, lettered from A up the channel, and gives each cleared base a check mark. A base further up that has not yet stirred is only an island on the chart. Matching flags above the gantries show distance. The flag of the next base to make for, the nearest standing one the chart shows, reads **NEXT** in brass. When that base is off the screen or astern, its flag waits on the edge of the view nearest the way to turn, with an arrow and its range, so you can always find it. A ring of coral dots round a heavy battery marks the water it can shell, and it brightens while the battery is ranging on her. The top-centre bridge display keeps the mission clock, clearance and stars in view while you pan or zoom, with the bearings of up to three bases the chart shows that still stand. A patrol sector holds three to seven bases up a channel between two shores. Beacon islands, pine islets and rock stacks stand in the channel and provide cover. Gold bars mark supply freighters, and small gold blocks mark coasters.

Every patrol is a passage up a channel between two shores, closed at its head. Breakwater enters at the mouth and fights her way up it reach by reach. A reach is four to seven hundred metres across: room to turn and fight, with land on both hands. Narrows between the headlands part one reach from the next, and the channel bends as it goes, so high ground hides each reach from the last. Each reach holds one stage of bases, one or two of them, each on its own island in a cove off one shore with a channel behind it. The two bases of a stage stand on opposite shores, and you take them in whichever order you like.

The bases of the first stage are stirring when you arrive. Those further up the channel sleep: their yards build but hold what they build on their slips, their garrisons ride at anchor, their floatplanes stay moored, their guns stay quiet and the chart does not show them. A stage stirs once every base of the stage before it is down. It also stirs, along with any stage before it, as soon as one of its own lookouts sights Breakwater or she shells one of its bases, so you can push on past a base, but not unseen. The bridge calls **BASE D STIRRING**, and its bearing joins the board. The light bases come first and the heavy yard is always last, at the head of the channel under a glacier. A cannery has one factory, the twin mills two and the heavy yard three. Each sea stages its bases its own way, one or two to a reach, and every sea of a round holds as many: three in the first two rounds, four in the third and fourth, and five after. Each round has a pool of nine seas, and each run charts three of every pool from its seed:

In the first round the three seas charted are the anchorages to choose from. After it they are the three passages each yard of the round before offers: a moderate one first and two tough ones after.

Each sea is held by one of three fleets, and every round charts one of each, in an order the run's seed draws, so the two tough passages are never the same fight. **Flotillas** are swarms of light hulls: bigger squadrons of patrol boats and destroyers, four torpedo boats at each base, and yards that launch about a third more often and never lay down a battleship. Their plate is light throughout, so many quick guns and the wing guns tell, and a big gun's slow round is spent on a boat a small one would have sunk. A **battle line** moors fewer hulls, most of them cruisers and battleships, keeps one torpedo boat at each base, and its yards stop laying down patrol boats once the raids are under way. Its battleships hang twelve-inch plate: a light round only chips at it, and a big bore or piercing caps go through, so it suits a few big guns. **Mixed squadrons** hold the rest as before. The chart stamps each passage's fleet on its tab and names it on its card, with the flagship's escort. About one passage in three past the anchorages hides a surprise the scouts missed: a battle line at the head of flotilla waters, flotillas swarming between a battle line's harbours, or either in mixed waters. It shows itself when its stage stirs: "BASE E STIRRING / SCOUTS MISSED A BATTLE LINE".

Sea cliffs run along both shores, with crags standing out from them and snowfields and mountains behind. Every run charts fresh waters. The run's seed decides which way the channel runs and how it bends, how broad each reach and how tight each narrows is, which shore each base stands on, and how the islands lie. Each sea keeps its character, and its card on the chart names its channel and the sea running in it. Open reaches are broad and nearly straight, with scattered rocks and beacons. An island sound is crowded with wooded islands. Narrows wind between pairs of islands either side of the course. A winding cut is a tight, crooked channel hard under the crags, through crowded stacks. Every chart is surveyed before it is sailed. The survey checks for navigable channels between the islands and the shores. Each yard and factory needs a clear shot from water the cruiser can reach. Each slip must lead to open water, and each yard needs two clear freighter approaches. The bases must stand at least 360 metres apart, each stage at least 380 metres up the course from the one before it, with the heavy yard last, and the course must pass each base within range of its guns. No mark on the course lies under the shore guns of two bases at once. A chart that fails is drawn again, and the best-scoring of the first three that pass is sailed. The helm trial keeps its fixed course. To sail a run's waters again, start the game with `SHIPVIVOR_SEED` set to the run seed from the log or a capture note. `cargo run -p shipvivor-encounter --example charts` draws sample charts of each sea to `work/charts/` with their survey figures, the current's streamlines and the ice.

The sectors lie in arctic water. Every sea has one or two glaciers three to six times the size of the other islands, under an ice cap whose glacier ends in a cliff at the sea. A glacier comes down to the water at one or two of the narrows, and another fills the head of the channel behind the heavy yard where there is room. The islands and the bases carry snow. Outside, a current sets along the coast and across the mouth of the channel, one way or the other. It does not run into a channel closed at its head, but the water of every reach turns slowly round on itself, in along one shore and out along the other, as the tide and the melt off the ice keep it going. The current is solved from the shores and the islands as a stream function. It follows each shore, parts round each island, quickens through the narrows and slackens in the lee of an island. Pilot's Cut turns hardest, at about a knot along its shores, and Greywater Sound is sluggish. No water anywhere runs faster than 1.25 m/s, so even the slowest flagship can make way against it. The current sets every hull, and the enemy gunners allow for it when they lead the cruiser. Wreckage drifts with it until the winch takes hold. Ice rides the current. Growlers are low lumps a few metres across. Bergs are domes, pinnacles or flat-topped slabs, with the odd slab 60 metres across. Ice stops shells and torpedoes and blocks the sight. Fire breaks it down a size at a time. One stock main-battery round splits a berg up to 20 metres across into two or three smaller pieces, the biggest bergs and slabs take two or three rounds, and a light round shatters a growler into brash. The pieces fly on along the line of the shot, fanned out either side of it, the smaller the faster, and glide about 30 metres before the current has them again. A torpedo splits any of them, and ice the current pins against a shore breaks up there. Ice splits with a crack, and a slab comes apart with the roar of a calving glacier. A berg outweighs the cruiser many times over, so ramming one at speed stoves in her bow and stops her, while nosing into ice dead slow does no harm. A ram at full speed splits a berg up to about 16 metres across and throws the pieces on ahead of her. Raiders steer round bergs. Ice turns with the water of its reach, and more drifts in at the mouth on the current outside. The mini-map plots every berg big enough to hurt, and capture notes record the current and the nearest ice.

Every roused harbour has a floatplane flying a circuit over its approaches. A sleeping base keeps its floatplane moored until its stage stirs. When the observer sights Breakwater within 320 metres, a **!** pops over the plane and the bridge calls its bearing. Offscreen alerts appear at the edge of the view. Tilt the bridge sight up to follow a high pass. A plane can see over low ground, lose you behind high ground and find you again. Destroying its home drydock calls the patrol off. [In-game view of the spotter and bridge alert](work/visualizations/spotter-patrol.jpg)

Base lookouts can sight Breakwater at 660 metres. Ship lookout range runs from 520 metres on a patrol boat to 900 metres on a capital ship. Planes and harbour lookouts send reports to enemies within 1,100 metres of the observer. Raiders head for the last reported position after losing contact. Shore guns can open fire at 320 metres, and a heavy battery at 1,600.

Two patrol boats and two destroyers are already at sea in the first reach. Each port has a drydock, shore defences and its own steel stock. The twin mills and the heavy yard have a second drydock to port of the first, with its own slip and quay, so each launches two hulls at once and takes twice the shelling to close. Look for the smoke on the horizon. Each working chimney sends up one thick plume that bends with the wind. A cannery is converted, with a timber derrick, one chimney and one single-gun turret. At the twin mills a blue conveyor links two tidal mills, with a twin turret at each end of the island and two slipways side by side. At the heavy yard, two triple turrets and a quad guard three furnace halls and two ochre travelling cranes. Their slings raise and lower steel plate over a lighter in each of two flooded berths. Foam washes through the granite ledges along the approach. Shelling a factory knocks it out for the round and its plume disappears. The enemy's flag flies over the slip for as long as the dock is open.

Each working factory supplies three steel per minute on top of a baseline of one from each standing dock. While a dock is down the factories put their work into raising it again instead, so only the docks still standing make steel. Each shore turret trains and reloads independently. The gunsight shows its remaining strength. A single has 6 points, a twin 12, a triple 18 and a quad 24. A freighter delivers six steel, repairs the worst-hit standing drydock and restores up to three points across its shore battery.

The heaviest base of each passage, the heavy yard at its head, also mounts a **heavy battery**: a larger gunhouse with two long 15-inch guns on the apron off its beam. It reaches 1,600 metres, five times as far as the light guns, which carries across most of the reach before it. It fires only while its base is roused and it has her, through its own rangefinder or its base's word, and high ground hides her from it as it does from a lookout. It is slow, one salvo of two shells every nine seconds, but each shell does 12 points, and four cards of armor plate cannot turn it. Its first salvo falls about 150 metres over or short of her along the line of fire. It watches the fall of each salvo and closes its error by half for the next, over, then short, then over, so on a steady course the columns walk in and straddle her by about the sixth salvo, and the first hits come at the fifth. A floatplane reporting her or another base's lookout spots for it: the first salvo falls within 90 metres and each closes by two thirds. A turn of 25 degrees or more since its last salvo, or a change of a third of her speed, throws the plot out and sets the error back to at least half a first salvo's, so weaving under its guns, or a change of telegraph, costs it its solution. Once it has lost her for 25 seconds it starts again. Like every enemy gun it leads her by her course and speed, and its shells take five seconds to come down at 700 metres and eleven at its full reach, so a change of course after it fires spoils that salvo as well. Its splashes are columns some 25 metres tall that hang and fall back wide, so the fall of shot reads from far off. Shoot it to silence it, as any shore gun: it stands 18 points, 9 in the first round. The round's cap on barrels applies to it as well, so in the first round it fires a single gun. The threat ring marks it with the star fort and its long gun, turned the way it is laid, and the sight names it as a heavy battery. A buoy circuit and the title waters have none.

Each drydock holds an order until it can pay for that ship and the berth is clear. A patrol costs two steel, a destroyer four, a cruiser seven and a battleship twelve. Patrols launch first. Destroyer orders unlock at 1:05, cruisers at 2:40 and battleships at 5:10. Each round after the first starts 36 seconds further along that escalation, up to three minutes along in the sixth round and at Crown Deep, so the sixth round has cruisers from the start and battleships at 2:10. Every enemy ship in a sector is either on patrol when you arrive or comes off a slip you can see. Two patrol boats and two destroyers lie on station along the course, each at one of three spots the run's seed picks, and each base keeps a squadron in its harbour, built on its own slips before you came. The squadrons grow up the channel and through the run, and each sea's fleet shapes them (`crates/encounter/src/doctrine.rs`). In mixed waters in the first round the base at the mouth keeps a patrol boat, the bases between keep a destroyer and two patrol boats, and the heavy yard a cruiser and two destroyers. They grow each round, then steeply from the fifth, until the sixth round's heavy yard keeps a battle fleet of three battleships, four cruisers and five destroyers. Flotilla waters keep more and lighter hulls, up to ten destroyers, three cruisers and a patrol boat at the sixth round's heavy yard. A battle line keeps fewer and heavier ones, up to three battleships, three cruisers and two destroyers there. Where the last stage pairs two bases, only the heavy yard keeps the heaviest squadron. The squadron of a base that is awake sails at once, and the rest weigh when their base stirs or when any ship of theirs sights her. A squadron sails as one, led by its heaviest ship, at the pace of its slowest. On passage the rest keep station on the leader's quarters, the heavier inside, or follow in column where the water is too narrow for that. What one ship of a squadron sees, all of them know. Once the leader has her in reach across open water, the squadron fans out into a line of battle across her bearing. Each ship lies broadside on at about four fifths of her gun range, so the destroyers lie nearest, out on the wings, and the battleships farthest, and the whole line wheels round her as the leader does. Sink or cripple the leader and the next heaviest takes the lead. The last ship of a squadron fights on alone, and the run log notes each squadron that forms line. When a base collapses, the next stage up the channel is scrambled: it goes to alert for a minute, its siren and flares go up, and it passes her position by radio, so its whole squadron sails straight for her and meets her on the way up, with its harriers aloft as soon as they may fly. Nothing replaces a squadron but the base's own drydocks. Everything after them launches from a standing yard. Supply freighters cast off from the nearest other yard still standing, never from the open sea, and sail unescorted. Two coasters lie at anchor in the roadstead off each base when you arrive, on open water clear of its slip and at least 275 metres from where you enter. They ride there until their base stirs, then weigh for the nearest other yard still standing. From then on each stirred yard that still stands sends two or three more to that yard every 35 seconds, while fewer than eight coasters are afloat in the sector. They leave its slip one at a time, and never while you are within 275 metres of it. A coaster bound for a yard that falls turns back and goes in where she came from, and a wrecked yard sends none. A coaster's trade is her own: she brings a yard no steel. Once a base is destroyed it launches nothing more, and a yard with no other yard left standing gets no more deliveries. A cannery builds up to destroyers, the twin mills up to cruisers, and the heavy yard can build battleships. A sleeping yard builds as an awake one does but launches nothing until its stage stirs, so each stage wakes with ships ready on its slips, and it may send freighters down the channel to the yards that are awake. Reinforcements launch at least 275 metres away. Aim at a gantry to see what is building, its cost and the steel laid into it. A badge rides over each drydock with the emblem of the hull on its slip, ringed by a countdown that a sweep eats clockwise from the top: the ring turns coral in its last quarter, and once the hull is funded and fitted out the badge pulses coral until the water off the slip is clear and she launches. Each slip draws steel from its base's store, the one nearest done first. Bringing down a gantry closes its slip and loses the hull on it, but the drydock is the base's primary target, not its only one. While any of the base's factories stand they raise the fallen dock again: 75 seconds with one factory, half that with two and a third with three. The badge then shows a brass spanner, its ring counting down the rebuild, and the sight gives the seconds left. A base is destroyed once every drydock and every factory there is down, and only then does it stop for good. Its shore guns are their own matter: they fight on until you silence them, each one silenced pays 4 experience, or you can sail out of their reach. The first drydock you bring down earns the drydock star, even if its factories raise it again. A factory that is knocked out crumbles in a run of crashes and falling brick. When the gantry goes, a fanfare sounds aboard at once, and the longer roar of the yard coming down reaches you a second or so later. A base that has you in sight or by report, or that is being hit, sounds its air-raid siren: two tones a minor third apart that wail as the motor is switched on and off. Like a gun's report it reaches you late, so a far base is seen to open fire before its siren is heard winding up. It keeps going for twelve seconds after the base loses you. When the base is destroyed the siren comes down with it, and its note drops away to nothing in about four seconds, which is the sound of a base that is finished. An alerted base also sends up three parachute flares towards where it last saw you. They burst sixty metres up and drift down under red and white canopies for a quarter of a minute, and another three follow every sixteen seconds while the alarm lasts. A boss sounds her horn as she puts to sea, and the Regent as Crown Deep opens: a steady, brassy note in prolonged blasts of four to six seconds, two of them for the Commodore and the Parliament and the three of a departure for the Regent. The bigger the ship, the deeper and louder her horn, as the rules of the road have it for real ships: about 162 Hz for the Commodore, 132 for the Parliament and 84 for the Regent. The note and the shape of each blast were tuned against recordings of big ships' horns. Like a siren it reaches you late, and the horn moves with her.

## Enemy fleet

Breakwater flies a flag of your own. Choose **Customise** at the title and the camera closes on her masthead, where the flag itself is the preview. Pick a pattern from the tiles, each shown in your colours, then one of its slots and a colour for it. Each slot's icon shows where on the cloth its colour goes, and 1–6 choose a slot from the keyboard. A pattern with fewer slots keeps the colours of the ones it hides, so you can go back. **Random** (R) spins through flags and stops on a new pattern and colours. **Done** (Esc) returns to the title. The flag is kept between launches in `flag.txt` in Shipvivor's settings folder (`~/Library/Application Support/Shipvivor` on macOS), or in the file `SHIPVIVOR_FLAG_FILE` names. The enemy hoists new colours on every leg, and again whenever yours change, always chosen so that at least half its cloth shows colours Breakwater's flag does not. Enemy flags fly over each open slip, at every raider's masthead, from a staff at a patrol boat's stern and on the twin standards over the Brass Parliament's gantry, and they stay hoisted as a ship goes down. A flag carries two to four colours in horizontal bands, upright bands, diagonal slashes rising from the lower hoist, a canton over bands, quarters, four triangles meeting at the centre, or an X with one colour above and below it and another at either side. The patterns can take up to six colours, ready for patterns to come. On a drawn flag, colours that share an edge always differ enough in brightness to tell apart at a distance, so gold never touches white and red never touches green. A flag you pick may break that rule. `cargo run -p shipvivor-ensign --example sheet` paints sample flags, each followed by four rivals, to `work/flags/sheet.png`.

Patrol boats close quickly with one deck gun. Destroyers are faster than cruisers and lightly built, circle close in at about 80 metres, and fire a single gun fore and aft. Cruisers circle at medium range and fire from two twin mounts. Battleships turn slowly and bracket the ship with three triple mounts. Coasters are small coastal traders, a quarter of a freighter's length, with a deck cargo of crates under a mast and derrick and their wheelhouse and funnel aft. They are unarmed and never come looking for you, and they are easy work: one stock main round leaves a coaster adrift, and a round from the wing guns sinks her. Torpedo boats are lean twelve-metre boats with no gun and a single torpedo tube, half again a destroyer's speed, and as frail as a coaster (see **torpedo boats** below). Every enemy gun, ashore and afloat, leads Breakwater by her present course and speed, so holding a steady course at speed no longer keeps her safe: turning or changing speed after they fire is what makes their rounds fall wide. Knocking a turret off a cruiser or battleship silences that station. Destroyers carry two secondary mounts, cruisers four and battleships twelve. Your wing guns, a pair amidships and a pair on the forecastle ahead of the forward turret until you fit more, work independently out to 160 metres, farther with rangefinders. A beam gun covers its own side. A bow gun covers from dead ahead round to its own beam, so the bow pair together cover the water ahead. Patrol boats, destroyers, cruisers and battleships drop packages worth one, two, three and seven stores respectively. Supply freighters carry four. Coasters and torpedo boats carry none. The sight identifies the class under the crosshair, and larger red contacts on the chart mark heavier ships.

Breakwater starts with 4-inch HE shells. HE bursts on whatever it strikes and never glances off, but it opens only light plate, whatever the bore. Patrol boats and cruisers are built of light plate throughout. A battleship's belt is capital-grade plate, and its deck and gunhouses are reinforced. HE can knock out a battleship's exposed director and slow its salvos, and strip its secondaries, but not much else. The armor-piercing shells card adds AP, which opens reinforced plate from the start and capital plate with piercing caps or a larger bore. AP can still glance off, and against light plate it goes clean through for half damage, which the sight reports as an overpenetration. The board beside the main battery shows which round each barrel holds once AP is fitted. Hits on the director stay local. A glancing blow sends a streak skimming away with a metallic ring. A square hit that stops produces a short flash, a low knock and a dent. A penetration throws fragments, vents smoke and opens that patch to later rounds of the same or smaller bore. The sight reports the outcome. Armor grades and flight times are tuned for the game's compressed sea.

Every patrol after the first builds up to a boss, and the passage chart names her. Half a minute before she sails, smoke goes up over her yard and a herald says she is raising steam. She sounds her horn as she puts to sea, deeper the bigger she is. She puts to sea 4:30 into a leg's escalation, so 4:00 into the second round, sooner each round after and 45 seconds sooner again on a tough passage, though never in the first minute and a half. Once she has sailed, the patrol is won only when she is sunk as well as every base. Look for the gold crown on the chart. She puts to sea with an escort from her own yard, onto the water off her bow, and they sail as one squadron on her: destroyers in flotilla waters (three in the second round, up to seven in the sixth), cruisers and destroyers in mixed waters, and cruisers then battleships with a battle line. The passage chart's card names the escort, and the stamps beside the round numbers across the head of the chart show each round's flagship: the Commodore's broad pennant, the Parliament's crown, and the Regent's crown and cross.

**The Steel Commodore** is the boss of the second and third rounds: a battleship lengthened to carry four gold-banded triple turrets, two superfiring pairs. She has about a fifth more gunfire than a battleship and a third more hull, and no turret screen, so she sinks as any battleship does, by flooding her.

**The Brass Parliament** is the boss from the fourth round to the sixth. Its twenty secondary mounts cover the flanks. Its ten gold-banded triple turrets fire in staggered groups. Each wing turret covers its own side, so clearing one flank gives you somewhere to sail. Aim at the gold roofs to break the mounts. Each one stands three stock rounds and falls to the fourth. A destroyed turret flies off and leaves a burning socket. The chart counts the remaining mounts. Her bridge, the tall tower amidships, takes hits even while her turrets guard the hull. Three stock rounds shoot it away. She then holds her course at about a third of her speed for 40 seconds, which gives you time to open the range or come round to her other flank. Her tag shows how long she has left without a helm. A bridge is lost only once. Once all ten turrets are gone the flagship drifts, its hull becomes vulnerable, and sinking it leaves a twelve-store package.

## Patrol and exits

Click an anchorage on the opening chart or press **1–3**. Each gives you five minutes to beat and three bases. **Lantern Bay** lies among wooded islands, with the longest interval between enemy launches. **The Iron Narrows** winds between the islands. **Blackcap Reach** is an exposed coast where reinforcements arrive fastest. The patrol clock starts when you choose.

Tap **Tab** at sea to unroll the passage chart. The patrol pauses until you stow the chart with **Tab** or **Esc**. The pause menu also shows the chart. It numbers the seven rounds across its head, from the anchorages on the left to Crown Deep on the right, with the three seas this run charts for each round down its column: the moderate passage at the top and the two tough ones below it. Your course so far runs in red through the seas you sailed. From your present fix three lines fan out to the next round's passages: solid where the stars you have earned this leg would open it, dashed where they would not. The rounds beyond run on as faint dashed lanes to Crown Deep. Each offered passage hangs a tab with its number, name, fleet stamp (three small hulls for flotillas, a large and a small one for mixed squadrons, one heavy turreted hull for a battle line), the most it can pay and a plane under air cover. A key under the chart names the stamps and the flagships. Each tab ends in a four-bar gauge of how hard the passage looks for your ship as she stands: one green bar for easy, then fair, hard and four red bars for deadly. A hard or deadly passage also says what would help her most, one or two of penetration, bigger guns, more guns, faster loading, secondaries, armor, AA or repairs: under the key at sea, where the chart has no cards, and on each passage's card at the yard ("Hard: needs penetration, armor"). At sea it reckons her as the yard will leave her, its repairs done. After drydock, click an open passage name or press **1–3** to sail it.

The gauge comes from an estimate in `crates/sim/src/outlook.rs`, not a battle. It sets her guns against the fleet that holds the sea: the hits each class takes to sink by bore and penetration (measured by shelling each class in the encounter), her rate of fire and the plate each round has to open, and the time and hull each base, its squadron, its slips' launches, its shore guns, the heavy battery, the flagship and her escort, and the air would cost her. It weighs that against her hull and what her crew mends on a passage. Small quick guns sink light hulls sooner and big guns battleships, so flotillas and battle lines read differently for each build. The hints are the changes that cut the estimate most: piercing caps, a step of bore on every turret, another turret, a rank of loaders, more secondaries, a rank of armor, a pair of AA guns or a mended hull. A fleet the scouts missed is a surprise, so the gauge leaves it out. The bands are set by a practised captain: a stock ship reads fair at the anchorages, and the rounds grow harder from there. [View the passage chart](work/charts/passage-chart.png).

Taking a tough passage, which needs three stars, brings you into enemy air cover in any round from the second to the sixth. At **A**, a red-roofed seaplane hangar replaces the cannery. Lookouts can call a strafing run from there. The chart warns of the aircraft's bearing and range as it comes in. Turn after it lines up to let the burst walk across your wake. If the last patrol was a tough passage, the station at **Crown Deep** alternates bombers and strafers. Shell the hangar or destroy its drydock to stop further launches. Moderate passages have no air station.

Two or three seas of each round's pool from the third round on keep an **airfield** up the channel, short of the heavy yard, and the chart card names it. Its harbour island holds a drydock and a gun like a cannery's. Beside its cove, on a flat island of its own, lie a runway, a taxiway and a row of five concrete shelters, each housing one aircraft, with two hangars, a control tower, the base's two factories and a fuel depot behind them. Four light guns and two heavy batteries ring the field's seaward side, so it has to be worn down from outside their reach before you close it. Once the base stirs, it sends up a wing of light bombers and torpedo planes about every 50 seconds: three in the third and fourth rounds, four after. The aircraft roll out of their shelters one after another, taxi to the end of the runway and take off, and that is the only time short of the air that your guns can touch them. The shelters turn every round, but an aircraft on the ground goes up at a hit, and your secondaries and AA engage any they can reach. A light bomber glides in across your track and drops a stick of two bombs where you will be. A torpedo plane swings out onto your beam, comes in low and drops a torpedo ahead of you, then breaks away. Each flies home, lands and taxis back to its shelter to fuel and arm again. The hangars build aircraft into empty shelters, faster with every factory standing. Bring the control tower down and the count toward the next wing runs at well under half its pace for as long as the tower stays down. While a factory stands it raises the tower again: in half a minute with both factories standing and the harbour's drydock across the water keeping the field supplied, twice as long once that dock is down, and twice as long again with only one factory left. Three fuel trucks shuttle from the depot to fuel what waits in the shelters. A truck goes up at a touch in a ball of fire and a small mushroom cloud, taking anything close by with it, and pays 2 XP. Without trucks an aircraft still fuels from its shelter's own line, only more slowly. The depot goes up in a larger cloud and sends out no more trucks. The base counts as destroyed once its drydock, its factories and both hangars are down.

Breakwater is built with a pair of light AA guns on her bridge wings, and the crew engages any enemy aircraft within 280 metres. Each gun fires a round fused to burst where the crew leads the aircraft to be. Bursts scatter about the aim, so a steady pass is hit often but not every time, and a plane caught in a burst takes its damage. A reconnaissance floatplane goes down after five hits and stays down for the rest of the leg, so its base loses its eyes in the air. A strafer or bomber takes eight, and its station needs 45 seconds to ready another. A stricken aircraft glides on as it falls and throws up a splash where it hits the water. A stock pair may take a few sorties to bring a strafer down. Against the full battery it rarely gets its burst off. Each enemy aircraft shot down is worth 3 XP.

Every base keeps a formation of light **harriers** moored in rows on the far side of its island from the quay, in plain sight. The bases at the mouth of a channel keep pairs, those further up keep more, and the heavy yard at its head keeps five: pairs, threes and fives up a channel of three stages. From 20 seconds into a leg, each roused base whose yard still stands sends its formation up, no sooner than 35 seconds after its last and only once that one is home, and only when there is room for all of it: never more than eight harriers are in the air across the sector. Nothing takes off from the open sea, and a wrecked base sends none. A formation comes in low and abreast, about 12 metres up, on short strafing passes of three rounds each, leading the ship as the strafer does. Between passes each harrier climbs away, circles out to about 300 metres and comes round again. After three passes, or as soon as its base is wrecked, it flies home and alights at the mooring. They are a nuisance more than a danger: three AA hits bring one down, and the first two cards of armor plate turn their guns. One shot down falls burning, trailing smoke, into the sea.

Every base also moors a pair of **torpedo boats** off the flank of its island, in plain sight. Once a base has been roused for 40 seconds and has word of the ship, it sends both out together, no sooner than a minute after its last pair left and only once both are home and loaded, and never while more than four would be out across the sector. Nothing comes from the open sea: a wrecked base calls its boats home and sends no more, and a base that loses a boat sends another off its slip 45 seconds later. Each boat runs in at 11 m/s, throwing up a bow wave and a white wash, to a point 300 to 380 metres off Breakwater's beam and a little ahead of her, where the water allows, puts her one torpedo in the water across your course, and turns for home at full speed to reload at her mooring for 30 seconds. The torpedo runs straight at 38 m/s and does not seek her. Its white bubble track shows on the water and as a red line on the chart. The boat's tube is heard coughing as it fires. A pip on the ring turns along the torpedo's run and points in while its track will pass close aboard, and the bridge rings its alarm bell about five seconds before it would reach her. Turn or change speed once it is in the water and it passes ahead or astern of her. Hold your course and speed and most will strike, though each boat judges your course for herself and misses now and then. A hit is a 16-point blow below the waterline that no plate turns, and it floods the module it lands on. Enemy torpedoes pass under their own ships, and land and ice stop them. A boat sunk pays 5 XP, but she carries no stores and does not count toward the raider goal, and her base soon replaces her, so chasing them earns little: dodge the torpedo, and wreck the base to be rid of its boats. The run log writes a line for each torpedo fired at her and each that strikes.

Each patrol awards one star for meeting the raider target, one for meeting the stores target, and one for destroying the first enemy drydock. The HUD tracks all three. In the separate helm trial, cross between the two buoys with gold lamps to advance the course. The trial HUD gives the bearing and distance to the active pair.

Destroy every enemy base in the sector, every drydock and factory, to clear it immediately, even if enemy ships are still afloat or shore guns still stand. The clock freezes on the winning hit. A short clearance view shows the last of the works collapsing before drydock or the final intercept briefing. Enter skips it. A damaged or empty yard can still take a delivery and recover. The clock is the time to beat, and nothing else ends a patrol. Each base destroyed puts 45 seconds back on it: the time swells and flashes green as it lands, and the bridge notice adds +45 S. In its last minute the bridge clock ticks aboard, soft at the minute and firmer as it runs down. When a base buys time back the ticking fades away over a few beats and holds off while 15 seconds of the clock run down, even if the time won leaves it inside its last minute. When it runs out she sails on and the clock counts up the time over in coral, until a base destroyed wins time back. Finishing with time on the clock pays a star's worth of yard points. Finishing past it pays nothing more, and the stars are kept either way. Once a boss has sailed she must be sunk too. There is no extraction.

Every leg pays yard points: two for each star, two more for finishing inside the clock, one for each enemy yard whose gantry comes down, and one for each whole point of cargo sent to the bottom. Each patrol ends in a drydock, where the crew makes good 40 hull and the yard sells its work for those points. The yard fits nothing unasked: every section is bought. Points you do not spend carry to the next yard, so each dock spends what the last left with what the patrol just sailed paid. Any level still owed when the leg ends, whether earned on the final tick or not yet chosen, is dealt as a free hand of cards before the yard opens.

Cargo ships carry cargo worth yard points: a coaster a quarter of a point, a supply freighter a whole one. Each hit on one puts cargo over the side in proportion to the hull it costs her, and whatever is still aboard goes down with her, so every cargo ship sunk pays her full load, part of it hit by hit. A crew that patches her hull cannot bring back what has gone. A cargo ship that reaches her yard takes her cargo in with her. Cargo ranks up into yard points on its own, apart from the level. The meter under the level bar in the BREAKWATER panel shows the yard's spanner, the points cargo has made this leg, and a cyan bar filling toward the next. Each time the cargo crosses a whole point, the spanner springs up and flashes, the bar brims white and drains to what is left over, and a small brass handbell rings once aboard. The points are banked with the stars at the leg's end, and cargo short of a whole point carries into the next leg. Crown Deep has no yard after it, so it shows no meter. Sinking a coaster also pays 5 XP. She carries no stores, so she brings no repairs, and coasters do not count toward the raider goal. A freighter still pays her four stores and counts as a raider. The run log writes a line for each yard point won from cargo.

In the drydock the ship is the menu. Hover over her to see each section's name, and click one to select it. Its refits hang below it, grouped by what they change, and a pale fan on the deck shows where each of its main turrets can train. Click a refit to pick it out and read its figures beside the list, and click it again, or its **Order** button, to order it. Work on the hull is picked out and ordered the same way, with its figures over the hull panel. The yard opens on the first bay that can take another turret.

Every order is made on the ship at once, drawn as a blueprint until it is built, and listed under its section with its price. Click an order there to take it back, with anything that waited on it. A second choice for the same turret replaces the first, so the price is always from the turret as built. Orders cost their points as they are made. The points left, and what the orders cost, stand over the big button at the bottom right. That button reads **LOCK IN** while orders are waiting: it, or **Enter**, builds them all, whatever refit is picked out. A sweep runs along her from bow to stern, and the blueprints become steel. Locked-in work cannot be taken back. The button then reads **SAIL**, and says how many points would be left unspent. The yard's panels grow on a large screen.

The **HULL** panel at the bottom left names her class of hull, shows her length and beam, and holds the work on the whole hull: a heavier class of hull and armor plate. She is built an escort, 5.5 metres in the beam, and the yard rebuilds her a class at a time: a **heavy cruiser** hull (7.5 m), then a **battleship** hull (9.5 m), then a **heavy battleship** hull (11.5 m). Each class costs twelve points, which is more than most patrols pay, so points are banked towards it. Each needs length first: 35 metres for a heavy cruiser, 40 for a battleship and 50 for a heavy battleship, so she stays at least 4.2 beams long and her bow still parts the water at full speed. A broader hull draws no more water than before, because the yard adds frame weight in step with the waterplane. Each class also caps her length: eight sections (40 m) for an escort, eleven (55 m) for a heavy cruiser and fourteen (70 m), all the basin takes, for a battleship or a heavy battleship, so she is rebuilt broader before she grows longer. A heavy battleship carries eight main turrets, three eighteen-inch barrels in a centreline turret where a battleship carries two, twelve-inch guns in her wing galleries and six-inch secondary turrets. She plates no thicker than a battleship. A tile that waits on a class of hull is padlocked with that class's name, and the hull tile strobes.

**MODULES** beside it is the yard's stock of whole five-metre sections. Drag one onto the ship and drop it on the seam where it should go: a cyan slab marks the seam under the cursor and the length it adds. The new section stays where it is dropped, ordered and drawn as a blueprint. The figure by the spanner in a tile's corner is its price in points, and it turns red when the yard cannot pay.

| Module | Points | Carries | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Turret | 5 | A centre turret | Four main turrets in all, six on a battleship and eight on a heavy battleship |
| Engine | 4 | An auxiliary engine | Two |
| Plane | 3 | A catapult and floatplane that bombs the nearest raider within 700 m, then comes back to rearm. Select it to add planes and bombs | One |
| Missiles | 4 | A box launcher that fires two homing missiles at the nearest raider within 1,000 m, every 20 s. Select it to add launchers and missiles | Two, on a 7.5 m beam or wider |
| Bay | 3 | Nothing yet: select it afterwards and fit a turret | Two |

The basin takes a hull of up to 70 metres. A new reserve bay is empty until you select it and fit a turret, which costs two more points. When a bay would leave too few for that, its figures warn that it sails empty.

A turret bay lists that turret's own bore, its barrels and its barbette, then the shells, loaders and training gear that apply to every turret. Each turret is built with a single gun and takes barrels one at a time: two points for a twin, three more for a triple and four more for a quad. Each step of bore costs more than the last: two points to 6 inches, then three, four, five and six, so 4 to 18 inches costs twenty. A wing pair is refitted alike, for one price. Fewer barrels or a smaller bore is free. A heavier cradle carries a smaller bore, so a turret takes no more barrels than its bore allows. Figures below are the largest game calibres in inches for each barrel layout. The bridge lists the secondary and AA guns, and an engine bay lists turbines, hardened systems and bilge pumps, each fitting two points.

A missile rack lists **Launch** and **Salvo**, and the plane section **Planes** and **Bombs**, two points a rank and two ranks each. They refit every rack, or the plane section, alike. Each **Launch** rank stands another trainable launcher on every rack: two abreast, then a third on the centreline ahead of them, all sharing the rack's gear, so a hit that knocks the rack out silences all three. Each **Salvo** rank adds a missile to every launcher's ripple, from two to three to four, and the box of cells on deck grows to match. Each **Planes** rank adds a catapult and floatplane to the section, two abreast and then a third ahead of them, with the crane moved to the centreline abaft them. The planes go up one after another and each goes for the nearest raider the others are not already after. Each **Bombs** rank adds a bomb to every plane, from one to three. A plane drops its bombs in a stick about seven metres apart, opened early so the middle of the stick falls on its mark, and holds its run until the last has gone. A stick laid along a raider can land every bomb, and one laid across her beam may land only the middle one.

| Cruiser · 7.5 m | 12 / 12 / 8 / 6 | 6 / 6 / 4 / 4 |

**Double turrets** are a pair of full-size turrets abreast on one section, for a capital beam only. From a cruiser's beam a bay offers them with a padlock, and the widen tile strobes to show what unlocks them. They take a centreline turret's cradles, not a wing gallery's, so a pair can carry 18-inch guns. Each trains over its own side and round to five degrees past the bow and the stern, so both can fire ahead or astern but only one on either beam. They fill the centreline, so a centreline turret beside them is masked across them and can be raised to fire over them, but the pair itself is never raised. Making or adding a pair costs four points, twice an exchange, and like a wing pair they are rebored and rearmed together for one price. A capital beam carries six main turrets in all, against four on the narrower beams. On a cruiser's beam with four aboard, the turret tile shows the capital beam as what it is waiting on.

A centreline turret beside another that stands as high as it can be **raised** on a barbette for two points a step, up to two steps, so a second or third turret in a row fires over the one ahead. The fan on the deck shows the arc it gains. Lowering it is free.

Each interior bay can be moved toward the bow or stern. Moves are free. Moving a turret relative to the bridge and machinery changes where its guns can bear. A bay is also where you add main turrets, up to four, or six on a capital beam. **Add centre turret** fits one on an empty reserve bay, and **Add wing turrets** fits a port-and-starboard pair there. **Split into wing turrets** turns a centre turret into a pair, which adds a turret without a reserve bay. Each exchange costs two points. A wing pair shares a single module, and each turret trains over its own side. Wing turrets take a smaller bore, and other centreline positions can keep their heavier guns. The bow, stern, machinery, director and final main turret are protected from exchanges that would remove them.

Click a refit or step through them with the arrow keys to preview it. Its price is on its **Fit** button, by the spanner. **Enter** or a click fits it at once, and she stays in dock however many points are left. The header shows the points left beside **Sail**. With nothing previewed, **Enter** or a click on **Sail** sails at once when nothing is left to spend. With points left, the first press turns the points red and the button reads **Sail anyway** beside them, and the second press sails. Any other choice in the yard stands it down. Fittings bought here stop at the same limits as the field cards. The encounter clock stays paused throughout.

| Passage from each yard | Required stars | On arrival |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moderate: the first on the chart | 0 | 30 more hull repaired. |
| Tough: the second and the third | 3 | A free hand of field cards, enemy waves 45 seconds further along that put out about a sixth more often, and a seaplane station. Bombers join at Crown Deep after a tough last patrol. |

Any sea of a pool can be charted moderate or tough, so the same waters come round again as a different fight, and the best-paid way differs from run to run. The stars are those of the leg just sailed. Each star, and beating the clock, pays 2 yard points from an anchorage, 3 on a moderate passage and 4 on a tough one. Every sea of a round holds as many bases, so the two tough passages always pay the same, and the moderate one about four fifths of that. The chart shows each passage's boss and the most it can pay: every star, the clock, and every yard, before cargo. Each round starts with fresh bases and a fresh clock: five minutes for the first three rounds and six after. The first two rounds hold three bases, the third and fourth four, and the rest five. Your ship, fittings, XP and run tally carry across. Clearing a sector brings her into a drydock, and sailing from it opens the chart for the next round. The sixth yard sails her on to the final intercept in **Crown Deep**. Press **Enter** at the briefing to sail as the yard left her. The crossing mends nothing more.

**The Iron Regent** is a 168-metre dreadnought, and she sends her screen of four cruisers and five destroyers on ahead. They bear down on you from about 800 metres, in plain sight but out of reach of their guns, and form their line of battle as they close. The Regent and her three battleships hold back over the horizon until two thirds of the screen is sunk, or for four minutes at most. Then she comes up the deep 1.4 to 2.4 km off, beyond the reach of every gun, with her horn sounding. A herald names her, and her gold crown rings on the chart. She comes up built against the main battery you carry: her hull, turrets and side armour are made stouter in step with your weight of fire, though not quite in step, so more guns still finish her sooner. A ship with no more than about 2.5 hull points a second of main battery fire meets her as designed. Break her twelve gold turrets to expose the hull. Her heavy turrets flash and commit to a patch of water three seconds before firing. Changing course during the flash can spoil the shot. As one broadside falls silent, she turns to bring the surviving guns to bear. The remaining harbours can send reinforcements, so knocking out a factory can buy breathing room. Sinking the Regent wins the run. She never escapes, and losing Breakwater ends the run on any leg.

### Simulated legs

`cargo run --release -p shipvivor-sim --example sim -- [stage] [first seed] [count] [temperaments]` has a bot sail legs on a fresh stock ship, through the same step the game runs, at about a second a leg. `stage` is the first word of a sea's name: lantern, narrows (the default), blackcap, outer, greywater, pilots, sealers, kittiwake, rime, whaleback, fogbound, gullet, longnight, hollow, splinter, frostline, tern, devils, boreal, calving, regents or crown. Each seed is sailed by a bold captain who never runs, a steady one who runs when badly hurt, and a cautious one who runs when hurt and from any crowd. Since the clock ends no leg, each sails until the leg is won or lost, or a minute past the clock; pass a comma-separated list such as `bold,steady` to choose. The bot steers for the nearest standing yard and runs in to about 210 metres off its slip. She silences its light shore guns first, then wrecks its works, and stands further off each time the shore catches her there. On the way her guns turn on any warship that closes within 260 metres, but her course holds for the yard. She leads her targets as the fire-control ghost does, fires torpedoes on either beam, combs torpedoes, and takes level-up cards in the order the run logs show a player taking them: loaders, then plate, then secondary range and the other secondary cards. Each second is scored as fighting, closing, idle, swamped (losing hull fast or with four warships within 600 metres) or fleeing. The summary gives each captain's outcomes, stars, lowest hull, damage per minute, hit rate, time crawling (with the engine down or held by the shore), the share of each activity, the longest quiet stretch, and how many legs had a minute of dead air, were swamped and then sunk, or ran and were sunk anyway. Each leg's log, with a pulse every 15 seconds, goes to `work/sims/<stage>/<seed>-<captain>.log`. A run seed sails the same waters with `SHIPVIVOR_SEED=<seed> cargo run`.

`cargo run --release -p shipvivor-sim --example runs -- [options]` sails whole runs. The bot takes her level-up cards and spends her yard points: the next bore, more turrets, a wider beam, more barrels, then plate and gunnery cards. She picks her passage and carries her ship from leg to leg until she wins or is lost. `--lanes tough` (the default) takes a tough passage whenever her stars open one, and `--lanes moderate` never does. `--skills` sets the captains, from 0, a novice, to 1, the bot at her best. A less skilful captain lays her guns less truly, more so the longer the range, decides more slowly and turns for torpedoes later. At skill 1 the bot sails rounds 1 to 3 much as the build 20 run logs show a player doing. `--pressures` scales every enemy blow on top of the game's own per-round table, `PRESSURE` in crates/sim/src/sector.rs (all 1 for now). `--shape` scales each round separately, to try a new table before building it in. For each skill the report gives:
- the share of runs won;
- the chance of getting through all eight rounds, counted round by round so that runs the bot could neither win nor lose are left out rather than counted either way;
- where the runs ended;
- the share lost in each round of those who sailed it, and their median lowest hull there.

It also fits a curve of that chance against skill, and works out what a field of players whose skills fall on a bell curve (`--bell mean,spread`, default 0.6,0.15) would make of it. Run logs go to `work/sims/runs/`. With the dev tools, `SHIPVIVOR_START=<round>` (2 to 8) opens a new run at that round, so the later rounds can be played without sailing the early ones. The bot first sails every round before it for real, which takes a few seconds a round in a release build. `SHIPVIVOR_START_SKILL` sets the skill of the captain who sails her there.

To hear the sound bank without playing, run `cargo run --locked -p shipvivor-acoustics --example audition -- tour.wav`. It writes fourteen scenes to a WAV file and prints when each starts: the chime for an upgrade, the handbell for a yard point won from cargo, the klaxon, the ship getting under way, an engine damaged and then knocked out and mended, the ship lying off an islet, a glacier and a working yard with a floatplane circling, the main battery firing among islands, a raider and a shore gun landing rounds close by, a heavy battery 1.1 km off walking four salvos in on her (over, short, over, straddle), a base shelled with its siren going until it falls, a base's siren and a flagship's alone, the three bosses' horns putting out 400 metres off and the Regent's again from 120, a floatplane flying past, and every sound alone at three ranges. With `horns` after the file name (`-- horns.wav horns`) it writes only the horns, and with `clock` only the bridge clock's last minute: its first beats, its last twelve seconds running out, and time won back with eight seconds left. With `hits` (`-- hits.wav hits`) it writes only rounds striking her, under way and then each alone: rounds her plate turns, from a light round's tap to a heavy one's deep clang, rounds that get through and burst inside her, and sections lost. `cargo run --release -p shipvivor-acoustics --example barrage -- barrage.wav` fires a heavily gunned ship's whole battery as fast as it loads while rounds strike her, and prints whether the mixer keeps up and how much of each blow is still heard. Building on Linux needs the ALSA development headers.

Every build logs each patrol run to `work/runs/<CODE>.log`, `cargo run --release` included. Build with `--no-default-features` to leave out the logbook and the ` capture key. The code is seven capital letters, shown in the window title. The log records each leg as it happens: ships sunk, hits landed and what became of them, hits taken and the damage to each section, bases stirring and wrecked, a heavy battery opening fire on her and being silenced, stores and stars, refits, routes and pauses. A pulse line sums up every 15 seconds of sailing. In Claude Code, `/recap` reads the runs of the latest sitting and talks them over, and `/recap <CODE>` reads one run.

A pale downward triangle marks a visible ship near the center of the view. Aim directly at a fitting to select that point. Pan onto open water to release the soft lock. X pins the selection and turns its triangle gold, even when you look astern. The chart panel shows the lock state, range and sighted fitting. In the Shift sight each numbered division is one target hull length, with half-length ticks between them. The lead cue estimates continued movement. In torpedo view the green fan shows the spread you will launch and a narrow white sector suggests a lead. The spread turns amber while that rack reloads. The run tally counts ship contacts from both gun batteries and torpedoes, including stopped rounds. Sunk ships and hits carry across every leg.
